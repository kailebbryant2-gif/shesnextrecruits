# Athlete Evaluation

How Coach Tay evaluates basketball prospects.
Labels and conventions: see `README.md`. **No invented thresholds.** Any numbers here come from her materials, with a source.

## 1. College coach evaluation criteria

#### EVAL-001 · What gets a player on the recruiting board
- **Label:** EXPLICIT
- **Principle:** She lists how to get on the board: "size, ability, shooting ability, versatility, being able to do defense, being strong, having a lot of athleticism, having great basketball IQ, the intangibles, like taking charges, you know, hustle, attitude, effort."
- **Applies to:** All positions and levels. No weighting or level breakdown is given.
- **Source:** [R10 @03:06–03:27]
- **Flags:** How these factors are weighed, and how they differ by level, is MISSING (Q-015). R28 "College Coaches Rank These Basketball Traits" (Batch 3) may answer this.

#### EVAL-002 · Get on the board, stay on the board, get the offer "punched"
- **Label:** EXPLICIT
- **Principle:** These are three separate stages. "Know what is going to get you on the board… what's going to keep you on the board to the next one… what's going to get that offer punched." Early on, coaches look for "hustling, having good attitude."
- **Source:** [R02 @12:45–13:09]
- **Flags:** What each stage needs, beyond hustle and attitude early on, is MISSING.

#### EVAL-003 · Players "still being evaluated" are judged on specific, fixable things
- **Label:** EXPLICIT
- **Principle:** Being on the board with no offer is "a great place to be at." Her examples of what coaches are watching: "can Benson really attack… can Sean really finish with her left hand… is Biel gonna fix her attitude… can Wilson shoot the three… how consistent she gets and how she progresses over this next year."
- **Applies to:** Younger classes on a coach's board (her example uses the class three years out).
- **Source:** [R02 @09:24–09:52]
- **Flags:** none

#### EVAL-004 · Waiting to offer = wanting more information
- **Label:** EXPLICIT
- **Principle:** Reasons a coach puts "wait" next to a player: to see if she gets better; inconsistent viewings ("I've seen her do really good, but I've also seen her kind of be really bad"); waiting on another similar target; or knowing she'd commit right away, so holding off to see if better players are interested.
- **Source:** [R02 @06:30–07:52]
- **Flags:** none

#### EVAL-005 · Coaches judge whether a player is at, below, or above their level
- **Label:** EXPLICIT
- **Principle:** "It's pretty easy to understand from our end if a kid is… at your level, lower than you, or… higher or better than your level… that's most of what recruiting is. It's like, where do they fit?"
- **Source:** [R14 @08:03–08:35]
- **Flags:** The materials don't describe how a coach (or a parent) makes this judgment (Q-015, Q-027).

#### EVAL-006 · The real test: can she help them win games?
- **Label:** EXPLICIT
- **Principle:** Coaches "couldn't care that much less about the recruiting board. They're thinking about how do they win games." Families should think ahead about the skills, versatility, strength, basketball IQ, defense and communication that level expects.
- **Source:** [R10 @03:30–04:50]
- **Flags:** none

#### EVAL-007 · How she responds to mistakes is evaluated
- **Label:** EXPLICIT
- **Principle:** College coaches want to see whether a player bounces back: "Is she going to bounce back, or is she going to have a bad attitude… does she just make hustle plays to make it up? Or does she sulk and then put up more bad shots?" That's one reason she wants AAU coaches who don't pull players after one mistake.
- **Source:** [R14 @06:59–07:29]
- **Flags:** none

#### EVAL-032 · Evaluation happens fast, and everything counts
- **Label:** EXPLICIT
- **Principle:** "Evaluation is about so much more than points and winning." Coaches watch "how she moves, communicates, competes, responds to mistakes, interacts with teammates, handles adversity, plays without the ball, listens to coaching, and much more." Impressions form "on the court, on the bench, during warmups, between games, and even how she carries herself around the gym." "You don't get to decide when the evaluation starts. It starts when the coach starts watching."
- **Source:** [EVALINTRO-T ¶1–7, ¶10]
- **Flags:** Says the specifics come later in the "athlete sections." Not received yet. R31 (video) is probably the matching walkthrough.

#### EVAL-033 · The academic questions coaches are asking themselves
- **Label:** EXPLICIT
- **Principle:** "Can she handle college? Can she manage her time? Does she take school seriously? Will she be eligible? Will I have to constantly chase her about assignments and grades? Will she be responsible enough…?" Academics are "a recruiting separator" when "everything else is close," though "it isn't always going to be the reason."
- **Source:** [M3.L1-T ¶12–17]
- **Flags:** Consistent with EVAL-029 (video).

#### EVAL-034 · Freshman-year evaluation is mostly projection
- **Label:** EXPLICIT
- **Principle:** Freshman year "is still largely about evaluation and projection": how good can she become, what does she look like in two years, what position, how much stronger and better. "Don't panic if freshman year isn't full of offers."
- **Source:** [R04-T FR]

#### EVAL-035 · Consistency is evaluated, especially when high school is her main exposure
- **Label:** EXPLICIT
- **Principle:** "One good game is not a recruiting plan." Up-and-down performances leave a coach asking "Who is the real player?" The goal: a coach sees her once ("I like her"), again ("She's consistent"), again ("I know exactly what I'm getting"). When a coach comes, show what you claimed (shooter → shoot; point guard → handle pressure and decide).
- **Source:** [R18-T ¶8–9, ¶13]
- **Flags:** Echoes EVAL-004 (inconsistent viewings lead to a "wait").

#### EVAL-065 · When a coach finally comes to a high school game: be someone they'd "watch again"
- **Label:** EXPLICIT
- **Principle:** At a high school game you get fewer looks than at an exposure event, so the goal of a single viewing is a memorable impression, not a big stat line. Don't "try to score all the points in the world." Show grit and the intangibles, and find a separator ("defend, dive after balls, sprint the floor fast"), something "that can stick in their mind." The standard to aim for is a coach thinking "okay, I would watch that kid again." One good game won't get her recruited. The mindset is "I need them to think that they want to come watch me again."
- **Her words:** "My advice would be to, um, not try to score all the points in the world, but, but show them a lot of grit, show them, um, all the things in the intangibles section, um, do find a separator, something that you're going to do, defend, dive after balls, sprint the floor fast, show them something. That can stick in their mind" [R18 @06:17–06:43]; "you want to do something that's like, okay, I would watch that kid again. That's the kind of impression you need." [R18 @07:05–07:11]; "Have the mindset of, I need, they need me, I need them to think that they want to come watch me again." [R18 @07:26–07:32]
- **Applies to:** All grades and positions, especially players whose main exposure is the high school season.
- **Source:** [R18 @05:56–07:32]
- **Flags:** Corroborates EVAL-035 and EVAL-028. *INTERPRETATION (labeled):* the written lesson stresses proving what you claimed ("If you've told a coach you're a great shooter, they need to see you shoot") [R18-T ¶8]. The video stresses intangibles and a separator over scoring. These read as complementary, not conflicting, but she doesn't say how to balance them. "Intangibles section" is a course cross-reference.

#### EVAL-076 · Film study as a separator between two similar players
- **Label:** EXPLICIT
- **Principle:** When two players have similar on-court traits, the one who shows she studies her own film (and brings it to the coach) draws the coach's attention. She reads it as being "bought in and dedicated." It's "another thing that not everybody is doing," on top of the practice, conditioning and recovery every teammate already does.
- **Her words:** "Let's just give the example of there's two of you. You guys have a lot of similar characteristics on the court. And you go to coach and you're like, yeah, coach, I was watching film about this. And I noticed X, Y, and Z about my game. I'm going to be like, oh my gosh, that girl, she's so bought in and dedicated. She's watching film. Um, my eyes are going to turn towards her more than the other one." [R21 @16:27–16:47]; "This is just another thing that I can level you up. Another thing that not everybody is doing and it can be a separator." [R21 @16:15–16:27]
- **Applies to:** All players, especially when she's being compared with players of similar ability.
- **Source:** [R21 @16:04–16:47]
- **Flags:** Corroborates EVAL-049 and MTH-030 ("recruiting players who can learn"). It's a tiebreaker of the same kind as academics (EVAL-029, EVAL-033). Her example is set in a team context ("you go to coach"). Whether a recruit should mention film study to a recruiting coach isn't stated (INTERPRETATION that it would carry over).

#### EVAL-091 · Sprinting the floor is S tier: "the best offense is not having to run an offense at all"
- **Label:** EXPLICIT
- **Principle:** One of the first things in her teams' first practices was "transition O," teaching which lanes to run every time the ball changes hands. Primary break first ("Having… an actual offense and having plays, all of that is secondary. We want to go primary"). Run wide and fast, "beating the defense down the floor," the 5 "rim run[s]" down the middle for over-the-top passes. Even without getting the ball, sprinting "opens up the floor for everyone else," including the ball handler, because the defense has to worry about the player already down the floor. For **shooters**, sprinting to get open for a three "spreads the floor out so, so, so much." It's "underrated, but coaches notice that kind of thing… an untrained eye wouldn't notice."
- **Her words:** "the best offense is not having to run an offense at all. So, being able to get the ball down the floor and make a layup and then step back up for defense. That's the ideal offense for a team." [R28 @02:13–02:25]
- **Applies to:** Every position. Shooters and the 5 (rim run) are named specifically.
- **Source:** [R28 @01:33–03:31]
- **Flags:** Corroborates BOXSCORE-T [R] "RUN THE FLOOR… coaches LOVE players who consistently run" and BOXSCORE-T S3 (sprint the floor → easy basket); HUSTLE-T ¶6 lists sprint-backs as effort stats.

#### EVAL-092 · Communication is S tier, and it's what makes a point guard
- **Label:** EXPLICIT
- **Principle:** Getting players to talk is a problem in "90% of… college practices going on right now" (her estimate). Players stop talking because they're out of breath and because they're thinking about their own job, "now you're into yourself," and "your teammates… can't read your mind." The skill is communicating while tired and while running and reading plays: "being able to voice what is actually happening." For **point guards** it's essential: "people like to call themselves point guards because they bring the ball up the floor, but a huge part of being a… point guard is being able to talk." That means saying what you're doing, what the other offensive players are doing, what the defense is doing, and relaying what the coach says from the sideline.
- **Her words:** "You can go into, I'm going to say 90% of, um, college practices going on right now. And the coaches are talking about how do we get our kids to talk to each other more?" [R28 @03:35–03:48]
- **Applies to:** All players. Point guards especially.
- **Source:** [R28 @03:31–05:06]
- **Flags:** "90%" is her own rough estimate ("I'm going to say"), not data. Don't present it as a statistic. Corroborates EVAL-019/EVAL-039 (point guard communicates constantly), BOXSCORE-T [T] TALK, EVAL-006.

#### EVAL-093 · Taking a charge is S tier: a momentum changer that needs no scoring
- **Label:** EXPLICIT
- **Principle:** Charges are "a dying art… kids just really don't take charges these days." A charge is a foul on the other team and "a momentum changer," which is hard to build in a back-and-forth game. It can "knock the wind out of" the other team, "you can feel that… air in the room change," and the crowd goes crazy. "You didn't have to score a point for it… You just had to be in your correct position, be willing to sacrifice for your team."
- **Her words:** "Charge, I wish there was a tier higher than S-tier, but S-tier for Charge because… it's like a dying art." [R28 @05:06–05:17]
- **Applies to:** All positions.
- **Source:** [R28 @05:06–06:05]
- **Flags:** Corroborates EVAL-001 ("the intangibles, like taking charges"), EVAL-023 (she averaged charges as a stat, R09), HUSTLE-T ¶6 (charges as an effort stat).

#### EVAL-097 · How a "cold watch" gets written down: positives, question marks, and the X
- **Label:** EXPLICIT
- **Principle:** In her walkthrough of a coach's tournament book (a fictional "Sunrise Team Elite 16U"), a coach watching a team "cold" writes down jersey numbers they like (4, 12, 32) and short notes, "obviously they're not going to be writing novels." Her example notes: #4 "good handles," "capable from three" (she may have hit one, or "her form looks good… Definitely looks like she's comfortable there"); #12 "good hustle," "great full court defender," "great rebounder," "good post moves." As the game goes on, doubts appear as **question marks**, not verdicts: #32 "missed maybe three left-hand layups" (rough day, or "she really doesn't use her left well"?); "Handles?", "attack?", "IQ? doesn't see lanes" ("she's just out there dribbling… she's got lanes to attack… And she doesn't take them… Is coach telling her not to attack?"). Question marks after one cold watch aren't a no: "None of this is gonna get you a complete no, right now." Coaches take notes in the book, on phones, or on an iPad. Back on campus, "probably Monday, Tuesday morning," the staff holds a **recruiting meeting** and compares notes ("so-and-so told me to go watch Nashville Sunrise Team Elite… let me go look at my notes").
- **Her words:** "none of this is, horrible, at this point, these are just question marks, right? This is a cold watch, first time out there. None of this is gonna get you a complete no, right now." [R31 @09:19–09:29]
- **Applies to:** Any player being seen at a tournament for the first time.
- **Source:** [R31 @05:47–09:29, 10:09–10:40]
- **Flags:** Notes are her illustration, not a scoring rubric. The book layout (rosters on top, room for notes at the bottom) is from her description; no image received. Corroborates EVAL-003 (fixable items like "can Sean really finish with her left hand"), EVAL-004 (more information needed → watch again), EVAL-032 / EVALINTRO-T ("EVALUATION HAPPENS FAST"). EVALINTRO-T is very likely the written intro to this video (its ¶8 describes it).

#### EVAL-098 · Redeemable vs. not redeemable: talking back to the coach gets the X
- **Label:** EXPLICIT
- **Principle:** In her example, a player who looked good (#4: handles, capable from three; "we needed a point guard") is dropped at the recruiting meeting because she talked back to her coach: "we're just not going to put up with somebody that is talking back… to the coach." That one moment ends what could have been a second look or a high school game visit: "Nope, no need. We're not even going to continue this anymore." Question marks still leave "a lot more room for grace… because everything has stayed on the positive," but behavior the coach saw "with my own eyes" is "confirmed." Players who aren't "a true, true standout, like separator" can't afford it: "I can go find another kid that… has good ball handling and is capable from 3, but maybe doesn't have like the highest IQ I want… That's not terribly hard to find. What is hard to find is… a phenomenal 3 point shooter that's a phenomenal ball handler and has a phenomenal IQ." Her list of things that get "the X": talking back to coach, walking back on defense, blaming teammates, talking back to refs, "not going full speed on any possession." **Redeemable:** turnovers, missed left-hand layups, missing lanes to attack, because a coach rarely offers "off of one look" and will come back "again and probably again and sometimes even a fourth time." The others are "a lot harder because you're not always gonna get another opportunity."
- **Her words:** "Those things are redeemable, right? … Taking back those… other little things are a lot harder because you're not always gonna get another opportunity if they're already gone. Given you that X." [R31 @15:19–15:47]
- **Applies to:** Every player, especially good-but-not-elite players.
- **Source:** [R31 @09:31–13:02, 15:02–15:47]
- **Flags:** The caption gap @09:37–10:09 means part of this passage is missing. The "fourth time" wording differs slightly from R28 ("at least three to four times") and R30 ("a couple of more times") (Q-066). ⚔️ Sits in tension with EVAL-003 / R02 "is Biel gonna fix her attitude" (attitude treated as something still being evaluated) and with R28's C-tier "bad attitude" (Q-067). Corroborates EVAL-045 / ALWAYSEVAL-T ¶9–10 ("an easy reason to cross your name off the list"), EVAL-021 ("superpower" / separator), MTH-011.

## 2. Athletic ability and player development

#### EVAL-008 · A weight-room progression from 8th grade
- **Label:** EXPLICIT
- **Principle:**
  - 7th grade: "a little bit."
  - 8th grade: get into a weight room with someone who knows how to teach lifting. Technique, not maxes. "Getting her frame ready to put on muscle."
  - 9th grade: intensity goes up ("It needs to hurt"), relative to her body: "intense for her might be adding fives to the bar or simply doing the bar." She mentions periodization: strong in summer, taper in fall, build again for summer ball.
  - 10th grade: "start pushing some weight around… we should know everything about technique."
  - 11th grade: train like a college athlete (hard lifts every day, recovery, nutrition, ownership).
  - Senior year: "college ready."
- **Her words:** "she needs to be getting her frame ready to put on muscle in the coming years… it's all a progress, and that's why it's so important that it starts now." [M2.G8 @00:21–00:48]
- **Source:** [M1.L2 @03:49–04:26], [M2.G8 @00:07–00:48], [M2.G9 @02:03–02:58], [M2.G10 @02:15–02:36], [M2.G11 @01:17–01:43]
- **Flags:** M1.L3 @03:27 gives a lifting frequency that's garbled in the captions ("2-2 days a week") (Q-020).

#### EVAL-011 · Become a complete basketball player
- **Label:** EXPLICIT

#### EVAL-036 · Development comes from details, not hard workouts
- **Label:** EXPLICIT
- **Principle:** From her own skills-training business: players "had done a lot of workouts. But they hadn't necessarily developed the details." "College players are made in the details": footwork, change of pace, ball control, passing, finishing, reading defenders, playing through contact, decisions, creating space, executing "when tired, pressured, or moving at game speed." Goal: "I got better at something specific today." Group training (1v1–3v3 competition) is valuable when done well. "Don't confuse more training with better training."
- **Source:** [R15-T ¶1–4, ¶9–12]
- **Flags:** Picking a trainer is in `recruiting-strategies.md` STR-037. "#1 skills training business in St. Louis" is her own claim.

#### EVAL-060 · A trainer is paid to critique. By a certain age she should be able to push herself.
- **Label:** EXPLICIT
- **Principle:** Being "drenched in sweat" is not the measure of good training. At a certain age, she should be able to put herself through a hard workout without a trainer. What a paid trainer adds is critique: spotting flaws, building up what she's getting good at, and encouraging her as she improves. From her experience with new clients who'd had other trainers, the common problem was "a lack of attention to detail."
- **Her words:** "a lot of parents would be like, well, she gets such a hard workout in when she works with so-and-so and she's, like, drenched in sweat. And it's like, okay, well, yeah, that's good, but, like, she should really, at a certain age, be able to put her own self through a really hard workout and be drenched in sweat. The goal of having a trained, trained trainer is to have somebody to critique you and point out these flaws and build up other things that you're getting really good at" [R15 @01:34–02:02]; "what I ran into a lot, uh, when I got new clients that had previously worked with a trainer is just, honestly, a lack of attention to detail." [R15 @00:15–00:25]
- **Applies to:** Families paying for skills training; all grades. "At a certain age" isn't defined.
- **Source:** [R15 @00:10–02:02]
- **Flags:** Corroborates EVAL-036 and STR-037. The age at which she should be able to self-direct hard workouts is MISSING (Q-050).

#### EVAL-061 · What detail-level coaching looks like, and the game-translation test
- **Label:** EXPLICIT
- **Principle:** Detail work means breaking a skill down, not repeating workouts in an area she's already good at. Her ball-handling example looks at what part of the hand she dribbles with, what she's thinking while she dribbles, how hard she pounds the ball, how fast she's moving while she pounds it, and whether she's changing direction. "There should be purpose behind it." Her test for whether ball-handling work transfers: can she go full court against one other girl without losing the ball off her foot? Hard dribble combinations are useful for improvement, "but that's not all ball handling is."
- **Her words:** "not just, okay, well, she's good at ball handling, so I give her a bunch of ball handling workouts that she goes through. No, it should be, what part of her hand is she dribbling the ball with that's causing this? What is she thinking while she's dribbling the ball? Uh, how hard is she pounding the ball?… Is she moving directions while she's pounding the ball? Like, it should dive so deep into every little thing, and there should be purpose behind it" [R15 @01:02–01:31]; "is it translating to a game? Like, can we actually just do it where she's, like, going full court with one other girl and not losing the ball off her foot? Does she have to be going pound, cross, between, between, behind, behind?" [R15 @06:10–06:23]
- **Applies to:** All positions, especially guards. Useful to parents judging a trainer.
- **Source:** [R15 @00:43–01:31, 05:59–06:23]
- **Flags:** Corroborates EVAL-036 ("college players are made in the details") and the R15-T ¶8 question "Can she use it in a game?" The specific diagnostics and the full-court test are new from the video.

#### EVAL-062 · Footwork is the foundation, and change of pace beats pure speed
- **Label:** EXPLICIT
- **Principle:** Footwork is "such a huge foundation of basketball," and many coaches don't pay attention to it, so a trainer who will work on footwork is a plus. Change of pace matters because always going fast fails in games: eventually she meets someone faster.
- **Her words:** "a lot of coaches do not pay attention to footwork and footwork is such a huge foundation of basketball" [R15 @02:09–02:18]; "change of pace, not just doing everything fast, fast, fast, because that doesn't work in a game either. Then you meet somebody that's faster than you and then what? So using speed, change of pace." [R15 @02:27–02:40]
- **Applies to:** All positions and grades.
- **Source:** [R15 @02:04–02:47]
- **Flags:** Both skills are in the R15-T ¶3 list (EVAL-036). The reasoning (coaches neglect footwork; the faster-defender problem) is new from the video.

#### EVAL-063 · Small-group training: 1v1 to 3v3, a peer at her level, and reps without waiting in line
- **Label:** EXPLICIT
- **Principle:** "One-on-one training" can be a small group with a trainer. Her formats:
  - **Two players (1v1):** a lot of live one-on-one.

## 3. Height, position, and physical attributes

#### EVAL-014 · Don't pigeonhole young players into one position
- **Label:** EXPLICIT
- **Principle:** "There are two ways to see the game of basketball": facing the basket (guard/perimeter) and with the basket behind you (post). Learning only one makes the other hard to pick up later. Young players, "probably even through, like, freshman year," should learn every position. Tall kids should still learn perimeter skills, and small kids should learn to play inside. Her own story: she was 5'4" in 6th/7th grade and "never grew another inch."
- **Source:** [R05 @00:28–03:19]
- **Flags:** none

#### EVAL-015 · Recruiting order by position
- **Label:** EXPLICIT (the order); ⚔️ ambiguous on where 4s rank
- **Principle:** In her words, positions are recruited in this order:
  1. **5s / true posts.** "You can't teach height."
  2. **Point guards.** Hard to find. Of "guards and smalls, a true point guard is gonna go first."
  3. **4s.** The "ultimate swing player." She adds: "I don't even know if I would consider these players as, like, necessarily 3rd on the list, because this is such a valuable player."
  4. **2/3 guards / wings.** "The biggest pool to pull from." "Last, definitely not least."
- **Applies to:** All positions. Explains why timelines differ.

#### EVAL-016 · Height can't be taught, so tall players go first
- **Label:** EXPLICIT
- **Principle:** There are only so many tall kids. "The hoop is ten feet tall, the closer the girl is to the hoop, the more the coaches are going to want her." Her comparison: a 6'4" player and a 5'4" player with the same "2 skill level." The 6'4" player "has a lot better chance of going to a school." Being tall and "completely terrible" doesn't guarantee recruitment "to the good levels."

#### EVAL-017 · Tall players should be capable inside, even if they prefer the perimeter
- **Label:** EXPLICIT
- **Principle:** Some tall girls don't enjoy playing the 5 (the physicality, the spatial awareness). She still advises training for it. Coaches "could even sell you a dream" ("you won't have to play inside"), and then on campus the player may need to play the 5 to get minutes. A tall player who can play the 3, 4 and 5 and guard both guards and posts has "so many more opportunities."

#### EVAL-018 · "Capable" and "swing" create value
- **Label:** EXPLICIT
- **Principle:** A player who can swing positions can be used against different lineups, covers injuries, earns more playing time, and builds "that value even beforehand in a coach's mind." Her comparison: two 5s up for an offer, and the one who's "not too uncomfortable on the perimeter" gets it.

- **Flags:** none

#### EVAL-019 · Point guards: valued for leadership and decision-making. Every guard should train for it.
- **Label:** EXPLICIT
- **Principle:** Point guards are "an extension of the coach on the floor." They control pace in loud, fast college games, handle pressure and criticism, see the floor, make decisions, pass, handle the ball full court, are vocal, make teammates better, and can "still be able to score a little bit." Any guard "should try to train to be a point guard." Being the only 2/3 "capable" at point "separate[s]" you. For undersized players, these largely intangible traits can set you apart. Her own reflection: at 5'4", not training as a point guard "hurt me." Decide early: "You can't decide, you know, junior year."

#### EVAL-020 · The 4 player: the ultimate swing player
- **Label:** EXPLICIT
- **Principle:** Often a slightly undersized post who can also guard the perimeter or shoot threes. Guards can't handle her size and post moves, and 5s can't guard her on the perimeter. She creates "huge, huge mismatches," which is "the most marketable thing." Tweeners ("not really big enough to be a post… not agile enough to be a guard") should train for both, not pick one and aim at a division.

- **Flags:** none

#### EVAL-021 · 2/3 guards: the biggest pool, so they need a "superpower"
- **Label:** EXPLICIT
- **Principle:** Wings are often the highest scorers, but there are so many of them that coaches fill other positions first. Interest may come later; "don't be discouraged." Ask: "What is your superpower?" Examples: defense, perimeter rebounding, drive-and-kick, shooting, running the floor, energy, communication, taking charges (her own example: about two a game kept her on the floor). Being memorable matters. Being "capable from point guard" is a separator. "You can't be 5'7", and just coasting around."

- **Flags:** none

- **Principle:** "If your daughter is 6'3"+, she is going to have a much easier time getting recruited than most players. And the taller she is, the more interest she is likely to receive." #### EVAL-039 · What a point guard has to do, and what "capable" means
- **Label:** EXPLICIT

#### EVAL-040 · The 4: playing the 3, 4 and 5 multiplies her minutes
- **Label:** EXPLICIT
- **Principle:** Small lineup → she plays the 5. Big lineup → she plays the 4 next to a true 5. With perimeter skill → minutes at the 3. #### EVAL-041 · 2/3 guards: a superpower, plus a fallback for when it isn't working
- **Label:** EXPLICIT
- **Principle:** Coaches "can afford to be more selective" with 2/3 guards, who are often recruited later. If she's not among the most skilled or tallest, find her superpower and build around it:
  - **Strength:** develop toward the 4.
  - **Speed:** develop point guard skills.
  - **Other options:** defense, rebounding, attacking, passing, vision.

  Then build a **fallback**: "what happens when the shot isn't falling?" Her coach's line ("If you're not making shots, you're pretty much worthless to me") came in preseason. "Figure out what makes your daughter hard to replace." College basketball is "Can you help me win?"
