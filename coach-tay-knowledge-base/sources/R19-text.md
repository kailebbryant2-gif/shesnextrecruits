[¶1] We talked earlier about the role your high school coach can play in recruiting. Now I want to take that a step further. Your high school coach can be one of your most valuable recruiting resources — but you have to give them the information they need to help you.
[¶2] Don't assume your coach knows: Which colleges you're interested in; What level you're looking for; Which schools you've already contacted; Which coaches you've emailed; Where you're planning to play; What your recruiting timeline looks like; What your strengths are; What you want them to tell college coaches. Give them the information. The easier you make it for your coach to help you, the more likely they are to actually be able to help you.
[¶3] DON'T JUST SAY, "CAN YOU HELP ME GET RECRUITED?" That's too broad. Instead, walk into your coach's office with a plan. You might say: "Coach, I'm putting together a list of schools I'm interested in. I've found the coaches' emails, and I've written an email you can send to introduce me. Would you be willing to send it to some of these programs?" Now you're not asking your coach to figure out your recruiting strategy. You're giving them a tool they can use. That is a completely different conversation.
[¶4] MAKE THE EMAIL FOR THEM. This is one of the biggest things I want you to take away from this lesson: Don't make your coach write the email. Write it for them. Give them a polished email that they can read, edit if they want, and send.
[¶5] EMAIL YOUR COACH CAN SEND. Subject: [Player Name] — [Graduation Year] — [High School] / Coach [Last Name], / I wanted to introduce you to [Player Name], a [graduation year] [position] from [High School] in [City, State]. / [Player Name] is someone I believe would be worth evaluating because of her [2–3 specific strengths]. / She is currently interested in learning more about your program and would love the opportunity to have you evaluate her. / She will be playing at [event/tournament] on [dates], and her upcoming high school schedule is attached below. / She is # [number]. / Here is her film/player profile: [Links] / Please feel free to reach out to me or [Player Name] if you would like any additional information. / Thank you, Coach [Name] [High School] [Contact Information]
[¶6] Notice what we're doing here. We're not asking the high school coach to promise anything. We're not asking them to say: "You need to recruit this kid." We're asking them to put their credibility behind an introduction. And that's valuable.
[¶7] GIVE YOUR COACH THE CONTACT LIST TOO. This is another place where families make things harder than they need to be. Don't hand your coach a piece of paper that says: "Here are some schools I'm interested in." Give them a spreadsheet. Something like:

| School | Level | Coach | Email | Why I'm Interested |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| School A | D2 | Coach Smith | coach@email.com | Location + style |
| School B | D3 | Coach Jones | coach@email.com | Academic fit |
| School C | NAIA | Coach Brown | coach@email.com | Program fit |
| School D | D2 | Coach Davis | coach@email.com | Position need |

Now your coach can actually use it. Make it easy to help you.
[¶8] YOU SHOULD BE CONTACTING COACHES TOO. Here's something else I want you to understand. Your coach reaching out does not replace you reaching out. You should be doing both. Think about it like this: YOU: "I'm interested in your program and would love for you to evaluate me." YOUR HIGH SCHOOL COACH: "I coach this player, and I think she's worth evaluating." Those are two completely different messages. And when a college coach receives both? Now your name has been reinforced from two different sources.
[¶9] WHY TWO PEOPLE REACHING OUT MATTERS. College coaches get a LOT of emails. Especially when they're recruiting. Your email can be good. Your film can be good. Your information can be good. And you can still get overlooked. Not because the coach doesn't like you. Not because they aren't interested. Because their inbox is full. That's why I don't want you to think: "I emailed them, so now I just wait." Instead, think about how you can create multiple legitimate touchpoints. You email. Your high school coach emails. You see them at an event. Your coach talks to them in person. You send an updated schedule. You send new film. You follow up appropriately. Now your name is appearing in multiple places.
[¶10] BUT DON'T SPAM COACHES. There's an important distinction here. We're not talking about having your mom email the head coach, your dad email the assistant coach, your high school coach email the recruiting coordinator, and your AAU coach email every person on the staff all on the same day. That's too much. We're talking about strategic repetition. If your high school coach reaches out to the staff, that can support your own communication. And you should understand that different members of the same coaching staff may have different responsibilities. The head coach may be making final recruiting decisions. An assistant coach may be responsible for evaluating your position. A recruiting coordinator may be organizing communication and recruiting information. So when appropriate, it can make sense for you and your high school coach to communicate with multiple members of the same staff.
[¶11] HERE'S WHAT THIS CAN LOOK LIKE. Let's say you're interested in a Division II school. You find: Head Coach — Coach Smith; Recruiting Coordinator — Coach Brown; Assistant Coach - Coach Jones. You might personally reach out to the appropriate members of that staff with your schedule and film. Then your high school coach can send an introduction to the staff. Now you've created multiple legitimate opportunities for your name to get in front of that program. And if one coach misses your email? Another member of the staff may see it. That's important. Because remember: You don't know which coach is going to be the person who notices you.
[¶12] YOUR COACH CAN ALSO GIVE THEM SOMETHING YOU CAN'T. There is another reason your high school coach's communication matters. You can tell a college coach: "I'm a hard worker." Your coach can tell them: "She is the first player in the gym and the last one to leave." You can say: "I'm a great teammate." Your coach can explain: "She's one of the best teammates I've coached." You can tell them you're coachable. Your coach can tell them: "She responds well to criticism and applies it immediately." Your coach can validate the things you're saying about yourself. That gives their communication a different level of credibility.
[¶13] BUT DON'T WAIT FOR YOUR COACH TO DO EVERYTHING. This is important because we've talked about this already: Your coach is part of your recruiting team. They are not your recruiting department. You still own your process. You should know: Which schools you're contacting; Who you've contacted; When you've contacted them; Who has responded; Which coaches have watched you; Which schools are interested; When you're playing; What your next step is. Your coach should be able to help you — but they shouldn't have to manage your entire recruiting process.
[¶14] YOUR RECRUITING TEAM SHOULD SOUND LIKE THIS. YOU: "I'm interested in this school." PARENT: "Here's the information and schedule, and we'll help keep everything organized." HIGH SCHOOL COACH: "I coach this player, I know her well, and I think you should evaluate her." COLLEGE COACH: "Okay. Now I have enough information to decide whether I want to watch her." That's how the pieces work together.
[¶15] THE ACTION STEP. Before you leave this lesson, do this: 1. Build your school list. Have the school, level, coach names, and emails organized. 2. Write your coach's introduction email. Don't make your coach start from scratch. 3. Give your coach your schedule. Include dates, times, opponents, locations, tournament names, jersey number and any relevant links. 4. Give your coach your film. Make it easy to forward. 5. Tell your coach exactly what you're asking for. Don't say: "Can you help me with recruiting?" Say: "Would you be willing to introduce me to these programs and send this email to these coaches?" Specific asks get better results.
[¶16] REMEMBER THIS. Your high school coach has a relationship with you that a college coach doesn't have yet. Use that. You have access to information your coach doesn't have. Use that too. You bring the plan. Your coach brings credibility. Together, you create more opportunities for a college coach to notice you. And remember: Getting overlooked doesn't always mean you're not good enough. Sometimes it means your name simply didn't get seen. So don't rely on one email. Don't rely on one coach. Don't rely on one tournament. Give yourself multiple legitimate opportunities to be noticed.
