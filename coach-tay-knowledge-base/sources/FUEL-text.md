[¶1] One of the biggest things I want athletes and parents to learn is this: Your body is the vehicle that allows you to do everything you want to do on the basketball court. You wouldn't buy a brand-new sports car and then put a tiny amount of gas in it and expect it to perform at its highest level. So why do we expect an athlete to practice, lift, run, compete, recover, go to school, and perform at a high level when we aren't giving her body enough fuel?
[¶2] THINK ABOUT YOUR BODY LIKE A CAR. Imagine you have a really nice car. You fill the tank all the way up. Now you have the fuel you need to drive, accelerate, travel, and get the most out of that car. That's what we want for an athlete.
[¶3] FULL TANK = MORE TO GIVE. When an athlete is properly fueled, she has more available to: Practice hard; Lift and get stronger; Run and condition; Compete; Concentrate in school; Recover between workouts; Recover between games; Build muscle; Maintain energy throughout the day. Now imagine you only put half a tank in the car. You can still drive. But you have less available. An athlete who consistently under-fuels may still be able to practice and play, but she may not be getting everything her body is capable of giving. Now imagine the gas light is always on. You're driving around on E, hoping you can make it a little farther. Eventually, that's going to catch up with the car. The same concept applies to an athlete. You cannot consistently run an athlete's body on empty and expect it to perform like it's fully fueled.
[¶4] STOP THINKING ABOUT FOOD AS THE ENEMY. Food isn't something your daughter needs to be afraid of. Food is fuel. Your daughter is asking her body to do a lot. She needs energy to go to school. She needs energy to practice. She needs energy to lift. She needs energy to recover. She needs energy to grow and develop. She needs energy simply to function. Especially for young athletes who are still growing, eating enough matters. This isn't about putting a young athlete on a diet. It's about teaching her how to fuel an athlete's body.
[¶5] AND NOT ALL FUEL IS THE SAME. Let's go back to the car. Some cars require regular unleaded. Some cars perform best with premium fuel. If you put the wrong fuel in a vehicle that requires something different, you aren't giving that vehicle what it was designed to run on. Your daughter's body needs quality fuel, too. That doesn't mean every meal has to be perfect. It doesn't mean she can never eat a cookie, chips, fast food, or a Pop-Tart. It means those things shouldn't be the foundation of an athlete's nutrition. A Pop-Tart can be part of breakfast. Crackers can be part of a snack. But crackers aren't a complete meal. A granola bar isn't automatically a meal. A bag of chips isn't a meal. And grabbing whatever is easiest every time she is hungry isn't teaching her how to take ownership of her body. We want her to start asking: "What does my body need right now?" That's ownership.
[¶6] TEACH HER TO FUEL HERSELF. One of the biggest goals as a parent isn't to make every meal for your daughter forever. It's to teach her how to make good decisions when you're not there. Because eventually, she's going to be in college. You won't be standing in the kitchen asking: "Did you eat breakfast?" You won't be packing every snack. You won't be reminding her to eat before practice. She needs to know how to take care of herself. She needs to understand: Protein helps me build and repair. Carbohydrates give me energy for activity. Fruits and vegetables give me important vitamins, minerals, fiber, and other nutrients. Water helps me stay hydrated. And she needs to understand that food isn't something she earns by working out. Food is what allows her to work out.
[¶7] WHAT SHOULD AN ATHLETE ACTUALLY EAT? Don't overcomplicate this. We want families to have foods they can easily keep in the house and athletes can actually prepare or grab themselves.
[¶8] PROTEIN. Protein is important for building and repairing muscle and supporting recovery. Easy options: Chicken; Turkey; Lean ground beef; Steak; Salmon; Tuna; Shrimp; Eggs; Greek yogurt; Cottage cheese; String cheese; Turkey or chicken slices; Rotisserie chicken; Beans; Edamame; Protein smoothies. Easy athlete combinations: Chicken + rice + vegetables; Eggs + toast + fruit; Greek yogurt + granola + berries; Turkey sandwich + fruit; Salmon + potatoes + vegetables.
[¶9] VEGETABLES. Don't make vegetables complicated either. Easy options: Broccoli; Green beans; Carrots; Bell peppers; Cucumbers; Spinach; Mixed greens; Asparagus; Zucchini; Peas; Corn; Sweet potatoes; Roasted vegetables; Frozen mixed vegetables. Frozen vegetables count. You don't need to make everything from scratch. If frozen broccoli takes five minutes to make, make the broccoli. The goal is consistency, not perfection.
[¶10] CARBOHYDRATES. This is an important one for athletes. Carbohydrates are fuel. Don't teach an athlete to fear carbs. Great options include: Rice; Potatoes; Sweet potatoes; Oatmeal; Pasta; Bread; Tortillas; Bagels; Cereal; Quinoa; Granola; Pretzels; Crackers; English muffins. Carbohydrates are especially useful around training because they provide readily available energy for activity.
[¶11] FRUIT. Fruit is an easy way to add carbohydrates, vitamins, minerals, fiber, and hydration. Keep it simple: Bananas; Apples; Strawberries; Blueberries; Raspberries; Blackberries; Grapes; Oranges; Pineapple; Mango; Peaches; Watermelon; Applesauce; Fruit cups. Keep fruit where she can see it. If she has to dig through the refrigerator, wash it, cut it, and prepare it every time, she's less likely to grab it. Make the good choice the easy choice.
[¶12] EASY ATHLETE SNACKS. This is where I want families to get really practical. Your daughter is going to be hungry between school, practice, workouts, games, and everything else. Have food available that she can grab quickly. Protein + carbohydrate: Greek yogurt + fruit; Peanut butter + banana; Turkey + cheese roll-ups + crackers; String cheese + fruit; Cottage cheese + fruit; Hard-boiled eggs + toast; Protein smoothie + banana; Peanut butter toast; Turkey sandwich; Chocolate milk + fruit; Trail mix + fruit; Granola + Greek yogurt; Hummus + pita; Edamame + fruit. Grab-and-go options: Banana; Apple; Grapes; Applesauce pouch; Greek yogurt; String cheese; Beef or turkey jerky; Granola bar; Trail mix; Pretzels; Peanut butter crackers; Protein bar; Chocolate milk. Again, these aren't "good foods" and "bad foods." We're building a fueling system.
[¶13] THE ATHLETE'S PLATE. You don't need to weigh every piece of food or count every calorie to teach an athlete how to eat. Start by asking: Where is my protein? Where is my carbohydrate? Where are my fruits or vegetables? Do I have enough food to support what I'm asking my body to do today? For example: Breakfast: Eggs + toast + fruit. Lunch: Turkey sandwich + fruit + vegetables + yogurt. Pre-practice snack: Banana + peanut butter. Dinner: Chicken + rice + broccoli. Post-practice snack: Greek yogurt + granola + berries. That's athlete nutrition. Not because it's perfect. Because it fuels the work.
[¶14] YOUR DAUGHTER NEEDS TO LEARN THIS BEFORE COLLEGE. Eventually, she is going to have to make these decisions herself. She's going to wake up and decide whether she's eating breakfast. She's going to decide what she gets between classes. She's going to decide what she eats before practice. She's going to decide what she eats after practice. And her body is going to tell the story. The athlete who understands how to fuel herself has an advantage over the athlete who simply eats whatever is available. That is another form of being MORE MARKETABLE. Because coaches aren't just recruiting what a player can do today. They're recruiting the person they believe can handle college basketball. And taking care of your body is part of being a college athlete.
[¶15] THE GOAL ISN'T TO EAT PERFECTLY. The goal is to learn. Learn what makes you feel good. Learn what gives you energy. Learn what helps you recover. Learn how to prepare. Learn how to make good choices when your parents aren't around. Learn how to listen to your body. Learn how to fuel for competition. And most importantly: Learn to take ownership of the body that has to carry you through your basketball career. You wouldn't put a sports car on E and expect it to perform at its best. Don't do that to your athlete's body. FUEL THE CAR. THEN LET IT GO.
