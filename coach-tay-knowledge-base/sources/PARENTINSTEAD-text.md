[¶1] In the last lesson, we talked about how: WHAT YOU SAY ≠ ALWAYS WHAT SHE HEARS. So now let's talk about what to do instead. I'm not going to tell you that you can never ask your daughter how many points she scored. You can talk about the score. You can celebrate a win. You can talk about basketball. The goal isn't to avoid those things. The goal is to make sure they aren't the ONLY things that consistently get your attention. Because your questions teach your daughter what you value. So let's start valuing the things that will actually help her become a better player.
[C1] CHANGE THE FIRST QUESTION. This is where I would start. Your daughter walks out of practice or a game. Instead of immediately asking: "How many points did you have?" "Did you win?" "Did you start?" "How much did you play?" Try: "HOW DID YOU FEEL ABOUT HOW YOU PLAYED?" Then listen. Don't immediately tell her what you thought. Let her evaluate herself. You may be surprised by what she says. She might say: "I didn't shoot very well, but I thought I played really good defense." "I was frustrated because I turned the ball over too much." "I actually felt really confident today." "I didn't play much, but I thought I had a good practice." Now she's learning to evaluate her own performance instead of waiting for you to tell her whether it was good or bad.
[C2] ASK ABOUT THINGS SHE CAN CONTROL. Instead of: "Did you win?" Try: "Did you compete?" "How was your effort?" "Did you communicate?" "How did you respond when you made a mistake?" "What do you think you did really well today?" "What do you think you could've done better?" These questions move her attention toward things she can actually control. She cannot completely control: The final score. How many minutes she plays. Whether she starts. How well her teammates shoot. What the referee calls. What the other team does. But she CAN control: EFFORT. ATTITUDE. COMMUNICATION. PREPARATION. RESPONSE TO MISTAKES. COACHABILITY. HUSTLE. FOCUS. That's where I want her mind.
[C3] PRAISE MORE THAN THE RESULT. Parents, this is BIG. Pay attention to what you praise. It's easy to get excited about: 20 points. A game-winning shot. A championship. Making the starting lineup. Getting an offer. Celebrate those things. They're exciting. But make sure you're also noticing the things that aren't going to appear in an Instagram post. Say: "I loved how hard you ran back after that turnover." "I noticed you cheering for your teammate from the bench." "I loved how you responded when your coach corrected you." "I saw you help that player up." "You didn't have your best offensive game, but your effort never changed." "I loved how much you communicated tonight." "You looked really locked in during warmups." "I saw you make the extra pass." "I noticed that you didn't let that mistake affect the next play." Those comments matter. Because now you're teaching her: THESE THINGS COUNT TOO.
[C4] NOTICE WHAT YOU WANT TO SEE MORE OF. Think about everything we've talked about in the Intangibles module. Hustle. Effort. Communication. Attitude. Coachability. Being a great teammate. Responding to mistakes. Doing the right thing when nobody important is watching. We told the athlete: MAKE THESE THINGS A HABIT. Parents can help reinforce those habits simply by noticing them. If your daughter dives on the floor three times and scores four points... And the first thing you say is: "Only four tonight?" What did she learn? Now imagine instead you say: "I don't know if I've ever seen you compete for that many loose balls. I loved that." What did she learn now? WHAT YOU NOTICE BECOMES IMPORTANT.
[C5] LET HER TALK FIRST. One of the best things you can do is ask a question... and then stop talking. You don't have to immediately analyze the game. You don't have to correct every mistake. You don't have to tell her what she should've done. You don't have to coach the car ride home. Ask: "What did you think?" And listen. If she says: "I played terrible." Don't immediately respond: "No you didn't!" Ask: "What makes you feel that way?" Now you're learning how she evaluates herself. Maybe she thinks she played terribly because she only scored six points. That's an opportunity to understand what she has started believing matters.
[C6] ASK HER WHAT SHE LEARNED. This is one of my favorite questions: "WHAT DID YOU LEARN TODAY?" Because now every basketball experience has value. Good game? Learn something. Bad game? Learn something. Win? Learn something. Loss? Learn something. Played 30 minutes? Learn something. Played five? Learn something. That mindset is going to serve her far beyond basketball.
[C7] AFTER A BAD GAME, SHE MAY NOT NEED A LESSON. This is important. Sometimes your daughter already knows she played badly. She doesn't need a breakdown in the parking lot. She doesn't need: "You know what you should've done..." She probably knows. And if she doesn't, her coach will probably address it. Sometimes the best thing you can say is: "Tough one. I love you. We'll get another chance." That's it. You don't have to fix every bad game before you leave the parking lot.
[C8] AFTER A GREAT GAME, GO DEEPER THAN POINTS. Maybe she scores 25. Absolutely celebrate it. But don't stop there. Try: "You were incredible tonight. You know what I loved most? You missed those first two shots and it didn't change your confidence at all." Now you're celebrating the 25... AND the mentality that helped create it. Or: "Great game. I loved how you kept finding your teammates when the defense started collapsing on you." You're teaching her to see the entire game. Not just the number next to her name.
[C9] DON'T MAKE EVERY CAR RIDE A FILM SESSION. The car can become a dangerous place for young athletes. They get in knowing the review is coming. What they did wrong. What Coach did wrong. What their teammate did wrong. What they should've done. What happened on that possession in the third quarter. And eventually they start dreading the drive home. Remember: YOU ARE HER PARENT FIRST. She has coaches. She needs you too. That doesn't mean you can never talk basketball. It means basketball doesn't have to be dissected every single time she gets into your car.
[C10] SOMETIMES ASK IF SHE EVEN WANTS TO TALK ABOUT IT. This can be incredibly powerful: "Do you want to talk about the game now, later, or not at all?" You're giving her some ownership. Maybe she wants to talk immediately. Great. Maybe she needs 20 minutes. Maybe she wants food and silence. That's okay too. Different athletes process competition differently.
[C11] HELP HER BUILD HER OWN VOICE. Ultimately, I want your daughter to reach a point where she doesn't need you to tell her whether she played well. She knows. She can say: "My shot wasn't falling, but I defended really well." "I wasn't communicating enough." "I handled pressure much better tonight." "I got frustrated after that turnover and let it affect me." "I didn't play much, but I stayed engaged and was a good teammate." "I need to get better at finishing through contact." That's an athlete who is learning to evaluate herself. That's ownership.
[C12] THE GOAL ISN'T TO SAY THE PERFECT THING. You're going to ask about points sometimes. You're going to get frustrated. You're going to say something in the car and later think: "I probably could've handled that differently." That's okay. This isn't about creating perfect basketball parents. It's about becoming more intentional. Before you speak, remember: YOUR WORDS ARE HELPING SHAPE THE VOICE SHE WILL EVENTUALLY HAVE INSIDE HER OWN HEAD. So reinforce: Effort. Growth. Character. Response. Confidence. Coachability. Hustle. Teamwork. Things she can control. And sometimes... Just remind her: "I LOVE WATCHING YOU PLAY." No evaluation. No statistics. No lesson. No correction. Just: I love watching you play. Because long after she forgets how many points she scored in that game... She'll remember how basketball felt with you beside her. DON'T ONLY PRAISE THE RESULT. PRAISE THE PLAYER SHE IS BECOMING.
