[¶1] When families start the recruiting process, they often make one big mistake: They think the goal is to find a Division I, Division II or Division III NCAA school and everything else is a backup plan. That's not how I want you to think about college basketball.
[¶2] NJCAA and NAIA schools can be excellent college basketball opportunities. For the right player, they can provide an opportunity to compete, earn a degree, receive financial aid, develop as a player and potentially create a path to another level of basketball. And in some situations, their rules and recruiting environments can give athletes opportunities that may not exist at an NCAA school.
[¶3] So don't eliminate a school because you don't recognize the logo or because it isn't NCAA. The goal is not the division. The goal is the right opportunity.

[J1] NJCAA: JUNIOR COLLEGE BASKETBALL. The NJCAA — National Junior College Athletic Association — governs two-year colleges. These schools can be a particularly valuable option for players who: Need more time to develop physically or skill-wise; Want to play immediately; Need a different academic pathway; Want to improve their recruiting profile; Aren't quite ready for the four-year level they ultimately want; Want to save money on college; Want another opportunity to eventually transfer to a four-year school.
[J2] And here's something important: Junior college does not mean your basketball career is over. For some athletes, it is the beginning of the next chapter. A player can use two years at a junior college to develop, play, improve academically and then transfer to a four-year school. That can be an extremely valuable path.
[J3] NJCAA HAS THREE DIVISIONS. NJCAA programs compete in Division I, Division II and Division III. And this is another reason you cannot simply hear "junior college" and assume you know what the experience will look like. The divisions have different financial-aid structures and different types of institutions. So when you're evaluating a junior college, you need to look at the specific school, program and financial package — not just the NJCAA label. The NJCAA currently has women's basketball programs competing across its divisions, giving athletes a wide range of opportunities.
[J4] WHY JUNIOR COLLEGE CAN BE SUCH A GREAT OPTION. One of the biggest advantages can be opportunity. At a four-year school, you may be competing with a freshman, sophomore, junior and senior roster full of players who already have years of college experience. At a junior college, your situation may look very different. You may have an opportunity to: Play. Develop. Get stronger. Improve your academics. Build relationships with four-year coaches. And then make your next decision. That doesn't mean every junior college player automatically transfers to a four-year school. It means junior college can give the right athlete a second opportunity to build her résumé.
[J5] BUT NJCAA STILL HAS RULES. This is important. Don't hear "junior college" and think: "The rules don't matter." They do. NJCAA athletes still have eligibility requirements, institutional requirements and rules governing participation and transfers. And because eligibility can depend on a student's individual academic and athletic history, you should always have the specific school and athletic department verify what applies to your daughter. This is especially important if she has: Previously attended college; Taken college classes; Played at another college; Participated in certain organized competition; Transferred; Taken dual-enrollment courses. Don't guess about eligibility. Ask.

[A1] NOW LET'S TALK ABOUT THE NAIA. The NAIA is another college athletics association that families should know about. And I really want you to stop thinking about NAIA as some kind of "lesser" version of college basketball. There are excellent NAIA schools. There are competitive basketball programs. There are great academic institutions. There are coaches who develop players. And there are athletes who choose NAIA because it is the best fit for them. The NAIA itself emphasizes that its schools can provide opportunities for student-athletes to compete while earning their degree.
[A2] THE NAIA ELIGIBILITY CENTER. One major difference parents need to understand: The NAIA has its own eligibility system. It is not the NCAA Eligibility Center. All first-time NAIA student-athletes must register with PlayNAIA, the NAIA's official eligibility center, and receive an eligible determination before participating in competition. So if your daughter is considering an NAIA school, don't assume that completing her NCAA Eligibility Center registration takes care of everything. It doesn't. NAIA has its own process.
[A3] NAIA ACADEMIC ELIGIBILITY. The NAIA's freshman academic eligibility rules are different from the NCAA's. An entering freshman can become eligible by graduating from an accredited high school and achieving an overall high school GPA of at least 2.300 on a 4.000 scale. There is also a traditional pathway where a student can meet two of three requirements: At least a 2.000 high school GPA; Rank in the top half of the graduating class; Minimum standardized test score of 18 ACT or 970 SAT. The NAIA has additional details and exceptions surrounding these standards, so don't reduce the rule to simply "you need a 2.3." This is another reason academics still matter even when you're looking outside the NCAA.
[A4] NAIA DOES THINGS DIFFERENTLY. There are several areas where NAIA rules can be different from NCAA rules. For example, the NAIA uses a different eligibility structure once an athlete is in college. A student-athlete can compete in four seasons during their first 10 semesters of college. Athletes generally must be enrolled in at least 12 credit hours and making normal progress toward a degree to remain eligible. The NAIA also does not have the same age limit that many families associate with college athletics. Its rules instead focus on seasons of competition and the 10-semester framework. That can create opportunities for athletes whose path through college doesn't look perfectly traditional.

[O1] AND THIS IS WHY I WANT YOU TO KEEP AN OPEN MIND. Maybe your daughter isn't ready for the four-year school she originally dreamed about. Maybe she needs another year of development. Maybe she wants a smaller school. Maybe she wants to play immediately. Maybe the financial package makes an NAIA school significantly more affordable. Maybe she wants a school where she can have a larger role. Maybe she starts at an NJCAA school and transfers. Maybe an NAIA school is actually the best combination of basketball + academics + finances + lifestyle for her. Those are all legitimate college basketball paths.
[O2] DON'T GET CAUGHT UP IN THE LABEL. I have seen families get so focused on: "Is it D1?" that they completely miss the more important questions. Instead, ask: Will she play? Will she develop? Does she like the coach? Does she like the team? Does she like the school? Can we afford it? Does the academic program fit what she wants to study? Is she going to be happy there? Is this a place where she can actually succeed? Those questions matter far more than putting a label on the school.
[O3] ONE MORE THING: RULES CAN CHANGE. This is especially important when you're dealing with recruiting. NCAA, NAIA and NJCAA rules change. And sometimes they change quickly. So don't rely on something a parent in your AAU program told you three years ago. Don't rely on something a coach told you about a different athlete. And don't assume the rules are identical across associations. Always verify the current rule with the governing organization and the school's compliance/athletic department. Your job is not to memorize every bylaw. Your job is to know enough to ask the right questions.
[O4] YOUR TAKEAWAY. I want you to remember this: There is more than one path to playing college basketball. NJCAA can be a development and transfer opportunity. NAIA can be a four-year college basketball opportunity with its own eligibility structure. NCAA Division I, II and III can be great options too. None of these paths is automatically "better." The best level is the level where your daughter can succeed — on the court, in the classroom, financially and personally. Don't let a division label make the decision for you. Find the right school. Then find the right basketball fit.
