[¶1] I want to talk about this one from personal experience. I owned the #1 skills training business in St. Louis, Missouri, and one of the things I saw over and over again was talented players coming into training who were missing some of the most basic skills they needed to play college basketball. They had done a lot of workouts. But they hadn't necessarily developed the details. And there's a big difference.
[¶2] Doing workouts isn't the same as developing a player. You can spend hundreds — or even thousands — of dollars on basketball training and still not be getting better in the ways that actually matter. If your daughter is doing a different workout every week simply because it looks difficult or exhausting, that doesn't necessarily mean she's becoming a better basketball player.
[¶3] College players are made in the details. Footwork. Change of pace. Ball control. Passing. Finishing. Reading defenders. Playing through contact. Making decisions. Creating space. Using the correct footwork in game situations. Being able to execute a skill when tired, pressured, or moving at game speed. These things are not always exciting. They're repetitive. They're sometimes tedious. And they're incredibly important.
[¶4] The goal isn't to leave a workout feeling exhausted. The goal is to leave knowing: "I got better at something specific today."
[¶5] So how do you know if a trainer is worth paying for? The first question I would ask is: Does this trainer understand my daughter's goals? A good trainer should want to know: What position does she play? What level does she want to play at? What are her strengths? What are her weaknesses? What does she struggle with in games? What skills does she need to develop? What does her current coach see as areas for improvement? What does she need to be able to do at the next level?
[¶6] Then there should be a plan. Not just: "Here's today's workout." But: "Here is what we're working toward, here is what she needs to improve, and here is how we're going to get her there." That is a completely different approach to training.
[¶7] Don't pay for difficulty. Pay for development. A workout can look incredibly impressive on social media. Lights. Music. Speed ladders. Ten different dribbling combinations. Players sweating. But none of that tells you whether your daughter is actually becoming a better basketball player.
[¶8] Ask yourself: Is this improving something she needs? Can she use it in a game? Is she getting feedback? Is the trainer correcting the details? Is the difficulty progressing as she improves? Does the trainer know where she's trying to go? If the answer is no, think carefully before continuing to spend your money there.
[¶9] What about group training? Group training can actually be extremely valuable when it is done correctly. Your daughter needs to learn how to use her skills against other players — not just against a cone. Good group training can create opportunities for: 1-on-1 competition; 2-on-2 situations; 3-on-3 situations; Decision-making; Reading defenders; Creating space; Playing through contact; Communicating; Competing; Making mistakes and adjusting. Those situations can be incredibly helpful because they begin connecting skill development to actual basketball.
[¶10] But again, the quality matters. A group of 20 players standing in lines and taking turns doing the same drill isn't necessarily better than a smaller group where players are actually competing and making decisions.
[¶11] And this is where I want parents to be careful with their money. Your daughter does not need to be in private training every single day. She needs the right training at the right time for the right reason. Sometimes that will be one-on-one training. Sometimes it will be a small group. Sometimes a competitive 3-on-3 environment will give her more value than another hour of stationary ball handling. And sometimes what she really needs is to take what she's already been taught and go play.
[¶12] Don't confuse more training with better training. Be intentional with your money. Find trainers who understand your daughter's goals, can identify what she actually needs to improve, and have a plan for getting her there. Because at the college level, it's rarely the flashy workout that separates players. It's the details they've mastered over thousands of intentional repetitions.
