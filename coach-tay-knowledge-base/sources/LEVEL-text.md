# LEVEL-T · Getting Real: The Honest Conversation About Level (written text)

[¶1] Parents, this might be one of the harder conversations in this course. But it might also be one of the most important. I talk to so many parents who believe: "My daughter is a Division I player." And I understand it. Because I was that player.
[S1] I THOUGHT I WAS DIVISION I TOO. Growing up, I was really good. In my area, I could compete with anybody I came in contact with. I had success. I could play. So naturally, I thought: Why wouldn't I play Division I basketball? But there was a hard truth I didn't fully understand at the time. I was 5'4". And I wasn't athletic enough to make my size irrelevant at the Division I level. That didn't mean I wasn't good. I WAS GOOD. But being good and being what a particular level is recruiting are not always the same thing. And looking back, I really wish somebody had explained that to me.
[S2] THERE WERE THINGS I COULD HAVE DONE DIFFERENTLY. We've already talked about this in the position section. At 5'4", I should have been working to become the absolute best point guard I could possibly become. I should have understood: If I want to play at that level at my size, what would I need to be able to do? I needed to make myself more marketable. Could that have changed my story? Maybe. Maybe not. I'll never know. But that's exactly why I want your daughter to understand these things while she still has time to do something about them. The point of knowing your current level isn't to put a ceiling on yourself. It's to understand: WHAT WOULD HAVE TO CHANGE TO GET WHERE I WANT TO GO?
[S3] YOUR DAUGHTER CAN BE REALLY GOOD AND NOT BE DIVISION I. Parents, this is where we have to separate emotion from evaluation. Your daughter may be: The best player on her team. One of the best players in her conference. All-State. A 20-point scorer. A starter since freshman year. Dominant in her area. And still not currently be a Division I prospect. Those things can all be true at the same time. Because college coaches aren't comparing your daughter only to the girls in your town. They're comparing her to players all over the country — and sometimes internationally. They're evaluating: Height. Length. Athleticism. Position. Skill. Speed. Strength. Basketball IQ. Versatility. Production. Competition level. Potential. Roster needs. And whether what she does translates to their level. That's a completely different evaluation than: "Is she good?"
[S4] ASK A BETTER QUESTION. Instead of only asking: "IS MY DAUGHTER A DIVISION I PLAYER?" Ask: "WHAT LEVEL IS ACTIVELY SHOWING US THAT THEY VALUE HER?" That's where recruiting gives you information. Remember everything we've already talked about. Who is responding? Who is consistently communicating? Who is coming to watch? Who is asking for film? Who is talking to her coaches? Who is inviting her to campus? Who is offering? Your recruiting tracker eventually starts telling you a story. Listen to it.
[S5] INTEREST FROM DIFFERENT LEVELS IS INFORMATION. Let's say your daughter believes she's Division I. You're emailing Division I programs. You're attending their camps. You're sending schedules. You're sending film. You're doing everything correctly. But the consistent serious interest is coming from: Division II. Division III. NAIA. Or JUCO. DON'T IGNORE THAT INFORMATION. It doesn't automatically mean: "She can never play Division I." She may continue developing. She may grow. Her body may change. Her skill set may expand. She may become more athletic. She may develop that superpower we've talked about. She may become capable at another position. Her recruiting could change. But right now, the market is telling you something. And pretending it isn't happening doesn't help her.
[S6] DON'T MAKE THE LEVEL PART OF YOUR EGO. This is important. Sometimes the athlete isn't the one obsessed with Division I. The parent is. Be careful. Your daughter's college decision is not a report card on how well you parented her. It's not proof that the thousands of dollars you spent on AAU were worth it. It's not something you need to brag about to other parents. And Division I is not the only version of success. The goal should not be: "HOW HIGH CAN WE SAY SHE PLAYED?" The goal should be: "WHERE CAN SHE HAVE THE COLLEGE EXPERIENCE SHE ACTUALLY WANTS?"
[S7] THERE IS GREAT BASKETBALL AT OTHER LEVELS. I really want families to understand this. There is really good basketball outside Division I. Division II has phenomenal players. Division III has phenomenal players. NAIA has phenomenal players. JUCO has phenomenal players. There are great coaches. Competitive programs. Championships. Packed gyms. Weight rooms. Travel. Teammates who become family. Incredible campuses. Great educations. And opportunities to continue playing the game you love. Do not treat those opportunities like consolation prizes. THEY ARE COLLEGE BASKETBALL. And for the right athlete, one of those programs may provide a much better experience than sitting at the end of a Division I bench just so she can say she played Division I.
[S8] GETTING RECRUITED AND PLAYING ARE TWO DIFFERENT THINGS. We've talked about this before. Maybe your daughter CAN get onto a Division I roster. Great. Now ask: Can she get on the floor? Because those aren't always the same question. Where does she fit physically? Where does she fit positionally? Who is already on the roster? What kind of players does that coach play? What does the depth chart look like? What role are they actually recruiting her for? There is nothing wrong with choosing the highest level possible if that's what she wants. But understand what you're choosing.
[S9] BE HONEST WITHOUT KILLING THE DREAM. Being realistic does NOT mean saying: "You're not good enough." I don't want that. It means saying: "This is where you are right now. Where do you want to go? And what would it take to get there?" That's completely different. Maybe she's currently projecting Division II and dreams of Division I. Okay. What needs to improve? Speed? Strength? Ball handling? Shooting? Decision-making? Position versatility? Athleticism? Defense? Exposure? Point guard skills? Now you have something productive to work toward. REALITY DOESN'T HAVE TO KILL A DREAM. REALITY CAN GIVE THE DREAM A PLAN.
[S10] GET OPINIONS FROM PEOPLE WHO DON'T HAVE TO MAKE YOU FEEL GOOD. This is where outside evaluation matters. Your daughter's trainer may love her. Her high school coach may love her. Her AAU coach may believe in her. You obviously believe in her. That's wonderful. But you also need people who understand college basketball and are willing to tell you: "This is where I think she currently projects." And ask them why. Not just: "Is she D1?" Ask: "What level does her game currently translate to?" "What is keeping her from the next level?" "What position would she realistically be recruited to play?" "What would she need to improve to change that evaluation?" Those answers are much more useful.
[S11] THE MARKET WILL EVENTUALLY TELL YOU. At some point, recruiting becomes less theoretical. Programs start showing you what they think. That's why we've spent so much time teaching you to track: Communication. Interest. Visits. Offers. Follow-up. The recruiting board. When multiple programs at a certain level are seriously recruiting your daughter... THAT'S INFORMATION. When programs at another level consistently aren't... THAT'S INFORMATION TOO. Don't take it personally. Use it.
[S12] DON'T MISS A GREAT OPPORTUNITY WAITING FOR A LABEL. This is what I don't want to happen. Your daughter has an incredible opportunity at a Division II, Division III, NAIA, or JUCO program. They love her. She loves the campus. She loves the coaches. She can play. The academics fit. The finances work. She can see herself there. But the family keeps saying: "Let's wait. Maybe a D1 will come." And sometimes... That great opportunity moves on. Because that coach has a recruiting board too. They need an answer. They offer the next player. And suddenly the opportunity your daughter actually loved is gone because everyone was waiting for a label. Don't let ego make recruiting decisions for your family.
[S13] I WISH SOMEONE HAD BEEN REAL WITH ME. I don't wish someone had told me: "Taylor, stop dreaming." I would never want that. I wish someone had said: "Taylor, you're 5'4". If Division I is what you want, here is what you're going to have to become to make yourself recruitable at that level." And then: "And if that doesn't happen, there are still incredible places where you can play college basketball." That's the conversation I wish I had. And that's the conversation I want you to have with your daughter.
[S14] DREAM BIG. TRAIN BIG. BUT SEE CLEARLY. Let her chase Division I. Let her dream. Let her work. Let her see how good she can become. DON'T PUT A CEILING ON HER TOO EARLY. But don't put blinders on either. Be willing to look at the player she is right now. Be willing to listen to what recruiting is telling you. Be willing to adjust. And be willing to get excited when the right opportunity comes — regardless of the letters attached to the division. Because ultimately, we're not trying to win the announcement. WE'RE TRYING TO FIND THE RIGHT COLLEGE EXPERIENCE FOR YOUR DAUGHTER. The right level. The right role. The right coaches. The right academics. The right financial situation. The right opportunity. And somewhere she can genuinely thrive. BE AMBITIOUS ENOUGH TO DREAM BIG. BE WISE ENOUGH TO SEE WHAT'S REAL. AND NEVER CONFUSE A DIVISION LABEL WITH YOUR DAUGHTER'S VALUE AS A PLAYER.
