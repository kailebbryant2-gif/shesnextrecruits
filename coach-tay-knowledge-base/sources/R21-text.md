[¶1] We've talked about how important it is to have film. We've talked about what kind of film to send. Now I want to talk about something that is just as important: Learning how to watch it. Because when you get to college, you are going to watch A LOT of basketball. And I don't just mean watching basketball because you love the sport. Film becomes part of your job.
[¶2] COLLEGE BASKETBALL IS MORE THAN WHAT HAPPENS ON THE COURT. College basketball is physically demanding. You are lifting. You are practicing. You are conditioning. You're playing games. You're traveling. You're recovering. You're going to class. You're studying. And then you're expected to come back and perform again. Coaches understand that they can't spend every hour of the day teaching you on the court. So they use another tool: FILM. Film allows coaches to keep teaching without putting more physical demands on your body.
[¶3] YOU WILL WATCH YOURSELF. College players watch their own games. You might watch: Your offensive possessions; Your defensive possessions; Turnovers; Shot selection; Rebounding; Ball-screen decisions; Closeouts; Transition; Communication; Defensive rotations; Plays where you made mistakes; Plays where you made the right decision. Your coach might stop the film and say: "What do you see here?" And they aren't looking for you to say: "I missed the shot." They want you to understand why the possession happened the way it did.
[¶4] YOU WILL WATCH YOUR TEAM. You'll also watch your own team. You might watch practice film to understand: Offensive spacing; Defensive rotations; Screening angles; Timing; Execution; Communication; Team concepts. Sometimes you will see something on film that you didn't even realize happened during practice. That's one of the reasons film is so valuable. You get to slow the game down.
[¶5] YOU WILL WATCH OTHER TEAMS. And then you'll start watching your opponents. Before you play another team, your coaches may give you film of that team. Now you're studying: Who is their best player? What does she like to do? Which hand does she prefer? How does she attack? Where does she want to catch the ball? How do they defend ball screens? What actions do they run? Where do they like to get shots? Who crashes the offensive glass? Who doesn't get back in transition? You aren't just watching basketball anymore. You're studying basketball.
[¶6] THAT IS A COMPLETELY DIFFERENT SKILL. There is a difference between: WATCHING BASKETBALL and WATCHING FILM. When you're watching a game for entertainment, you might notice: "Wow, that was a great shot." When you're watching film as a player, you should be asking: "Why did she get that shot?" That's the difference. You are looking for patterns. You're looking for decisions. You're looking for tendencies. You're looking for opportunities.
[¶7] START LEARNING THIS NOW. You don't have to wait until college to learn how to watch film. In fact, I want you to start now. Take one of your own full games. Don't watch it like a fan. Watch it like a coach. And start asking questions.
[¶8] OFFENSE: Where did I get my shots? Were they good shots? Did I make the right read? Did I move when I didn't have the ball? Did I attack closeouts? Did I make the extra pass? Did I turn the ball over unnecessarily?
DEFENSE: Did I communicate? Did I get beat? Why did I get beat? Was I in the correct position? Did I help when I was supposed to? Did I close out under control? Did I box out?
TRANSITION: Did I sprint? Did I get back? Did I run the floor? Did I find someone to match up with?
INTANGIBLES: Did I encourage teammates? Did I respond after a mistake? Did I communicate with teammates? Did I stay engaged on the bench? Did my body language change when things weren't going well?
You're learning to see yourself. That's a skill.
[¶9] DON'T JUST WATCH THE BALL. This is one of the biggest mistakes young players make. They watch the ball. But some of the most important things happening in basketball are happening away from the ball. Watch yourself when you don't have it. Watch the weak side. Watch the screener. Watch the defender who is helping. Watch the spacing. Watch what happens before the shot. Ask: What caused this opportunity? That's where your basketball IQ starts to grow.
[¶10] WATCH YOURSELF LIKE A COACH. Here's an exercise I really want you to try. Watch one full game and pretend you are the college coach evaluating yourself. Don't make excuses. Don't say: "I was tired." "She fouled me." "The pass was bad." "I normally make that." Instead ask: What actually happened? Then ask: What could I have done better? That's how you improve.
[¶11] THEN WATCH A GAME THAT ISN'T YOURS. This is another great exercise. Watch a college game. Pick one player who plays your position. Don't watch the entire game like a fan. Watch that player. Ask: Where does she stand? How does she move without the ball? How does she defend? How quickly does she make decisions? What does she do when she isn't scoring? How does she communicate? How does she rebound? How does she create advantages? You may start noticing: "She barely touched the ball for three possessions, but she was still impacting the game." That's an important lesson. Because remember what we've talked about throughout this course: Being valuable is about more than scoring. Film helps you actually see that.
[¶12] THIS WILL MAKE YOU MORE MARKETABLE. Here's why I included this lesson in a recruiting course. Because I want you to understand that being recruitable is about more than your physical talent. A coach wants players who can learn. Players who can be coached. Players who can understand the game. Players who can identify their mistakes. Players who can make adjustments. Players who can understand an opponent. Players who can take information from a coach and apply it. If you already know how to watch film when you arrive on campus, you've already developed a skill that another young player may not have. That's another way you become MORE MARKETABLE.
[¶13] YOUR GOAL ISN'T TO KNOW EVERYTHING. You aren't supposed to watch film at 13, 14, 15 or 16 years old and suddenly have a college coach's basketball IQ. That's not the point. The point is to start developing the habit. Start learning how to ask better questions. Start learning how to recognize patterns. Start learning how to watch yourself honestly. Start learning how to watch other players. Then, when you get to college and your coach puts film in front of you and says: "Let's watch." You're not thinking: "Why are we watching this?" You're thinking: "Okay. What am I supposed to learn from this?" That's a completely different mindset.
[¶14] THE PLAYER WHO LEARNS OFF THE COURT HAS AN ADVANTAGE. Remember: You only have so many hours in the day. You can't spend every hour on the court. Your body needs recovery. Your muscles need recovery. Your mind needs recovery. But you can still learn. Film allows you to keep developing your basketball brain without putting more physical stress on your body. That's why college coaches use it so much. And that's why I want you learning how to use it now.
[¶15] DON'T JUST WATCH BASKETBALL. LEARN TO STUDY IT. The earlier you learn how to watch yourself, your teammates and your opponents with a coach's eye, the more prepared you'll be for the college game. And more importantly: You're developing another skill that makes you more marketable. Because coaches aren't just recruiting athletes. They're recruiting players who can learn.
