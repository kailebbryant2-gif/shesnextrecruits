[¶1] This is one of the biggest things I want parents and athletes to understand because interest and an offer are very, very different things. And if you don't understand that difference, it is incredibly easy to get excited about the wrong things. Let's make this as simple as possible.
[I1] BEFORE AN OFFER = INTEREST. Any conversation that happens before an offer is interest. That means: A coach calls you; A coach texts you; A coach emails you; A coach follows you; A coach sends you something in the mail; A coach invites you to a camp; A coach invites you to campus; A coach asks your coach about you; A coach talks to your parents; A coach watches you play; A coach tells you they really like you; A coach tells you that you're a great fit; A coach tells you that you're one of their top players; A coach tells you they want to keep watching you; A coach tells you they're recruiting you; A coach contacts your AAU or high school coach.
[I2] All of that is interest. It can be meaningful interest. It can be strong interest. It can be genuine interest. But until they actually offer you a spot and scholarship, it is still interest.
[I3] TAKE INTEREST FOR WHAT IT IS. Interest. I know that's difficult to hear when it is a school your daughter really wants. Maybe it's her dream school. Maybe she has been talking to that coach for six months. Maybe the coach tells her how much they love her game. Maybe they are texting her every week. Maybe she gets invited to campus. That can all feel like recruiting is basically done. It isn't. Until there is an offer, there is no offer.
[O1] SO WHAT DOES AN ACTUAL OFFER LOOK LIKE? An actual offer should be a very clear conversation. The coach should plainly tell your daughter: "We are offering you a scholarship." This should not be something you have to decode. You shouldn't have to leave a phone call thinking: "Wait...did they actually offer her?" You should know. And you should know what the offer means.
[O2] The coach should be able to explain things like: Is it a full scholarship? Is it a partial scholarship? What does the athletic scholarship cover? What would your family be responsible for paying? Is there academic money involved? Are there other forms of financial aid being considered? Are there conditions that affect the amount? What does the financial package look like based on the information they currently have?
[O3] There can absolutely be pieces of the financial package that depend on things like academics, financial aid, or other circumstances. But for the most part, the coach should be able to clearly explain what they are offering and what your daughter would be responsible for. If you don't understand the offer, ask questions. Don't be afraid to say: "Can you walk us through exactly what the scholarship covers and what our expected cost would be?" That's a completely reasonable question.
[F1] INTEREST CAN FEEL LIKE AN OFFER. This is where families get into trouble. A coach can make your daughter feel incredibly wanted without actually offering her a spot. And sometimes the coach genuinely does like her. They may really be interested. But interest is not commitment. Think about it this way: INTEREST: "We really like you." OFFER: "We want you here." Those are different conversations. A coach can like 50 players. They cannot put 50 players on a roster if they only have a certain number of spots. That's why you have to pay attention to what has actually happened — not just how exciting the communication feels.
[F2] DON'T LET YOUR FAVORITE SCHOOL BLIND YOU. This is one of the biggest pieces of advice I can give you. Do not let interest from your favorite school become more valuable to you than an actual offer from another school. Let's say your daughter desperately wants School A. School A has been texting her. They've watched her. They've invited her to campus. They tell her she's one of their favorite players. Everyone is excited. But they haven't offered her. Meanwhile, School B — a school your daughter wasn't initially as excited about — offers her a scholarship. Don't automatically turn your nose up at School B because School A is showing more interest. School B has actually made a commitment. School A has not. That doesn't mean School A isn't interested. It means they haven't made the same commitment. And you need to treat those situations differently.
[F3] THIS IS WHERE FAMILIES MISS OPPORTUNITIES. I've seen players get so focused on the school they hope will offer that they ignore the school that actually did. They keep waiting. They assume the offer is coming. They turn down opportunities because: "But we really like this other school." And then sometimes the other school never offers. Now the player has potentially passed up a great opportunity waiting for something that was never guaranteed. That's why I want you to separate your emotions from the information. You can absolutely love a school. You can absolutely hope they offer. You can absolutely continue building that relationship. But you should still be honest with yourself about where you actually stand.
[F4] HERE'S HOW I WANT YOU TO THINK ABOUT IT. Interest means: "We are considering you." An offer means: "We have decided we want you." That's a major difference. And when you are comparing schools, don't let a long list of exciting conversations outweigh one real opportunity.
[F5] DON'T COUNT CONTACT. COUNT COMMITMENT. Contact tells you that a coach is paying attention. An offer tells you that the program is willing to commit a roster spot and scholarship opportunity to your daughter. Those are not the same thing.
[Q1] THE QUESTIONS I WANT YOU TO ASK. Instead of saying: "She has so much interest!" Start asking: "Has she been offered?" If the answer is no, then you know exactly where you stand. And if the answer is yes, then start asking: "What exactly are they offering?" "What does the scholarship cover?" "What would our cost be?" "What is the expected role?" "How many players are they bringing in?" "What is the timeline for making a decision?" Now you're having a real recruiting conversation.
[T1] BIG TAKEAWAY. I want you to get excited about interest. Interest is good. It means a coach sees something in your daughter. But don't turn interest into something it isn't. Don't tell yourself an offer is coming because a coach is texting. Don't assume a campus invitation means a scholarship is next. Don't assume being called a "top recruit" means there is a roster spot waiting. And don't let interest from your dream school cause you to overlook an actual opportunity somewhere else. Interest is interest. An offer is an offer. Know the difference. Because when it comes time to make decisions, you need to make them based on what has actually been offered — not what you hope might happen.
