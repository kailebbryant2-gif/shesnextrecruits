[¶1] Parents, this section is for you. And I want to start with something that has almost nothing to do with recruiting. It has to do with the conversations you're having with your daughter every single day. After practice. After games. On the drive home. At dinner. Walking out of the gym. And I want you to think about one question: WHAT IS THE FIRST THING YOU USUALLY ASK? Maybe it's: "Did you win?" "How many points did you have?" "Did you start?" "How many minutes did you play?" "How did you play?" "Who scored the most?" "Did you beat that good team?" These probably don't feel like harmful questions. And most of the time, they're not coming from a bad place at all. You're interested. You care. You want to know what happened. You want to celebrate her. But here's what I want parents to understand: WHAT YOU SAY AND WHAT SHE HEARS CAN BE TWO COMPLETELY DIFFERENT THINGS.
[Q1] "HOW MANY POINTS DID YOU HAVE?" WHAT YOU SAY: "How many points did you have?" WHAT YOU MEAN: I want to hear about your game. WHAT SHE MAY HEAR: Scoring is how we decide whether I played well. Now imagine she hears some version of that question after hundreds of games. Eventually she may start evaluating herself the same way. 15 points = I played great. 2 points = I played terrible. But what if she had 2 points and completely changed the game defensively? What if she had eight deflections? What if she guarded the other team's best player? What if she communicated all night? What if she made the pass that led to the pass that created the basket? What if she played the best basketball game of her life without scoring much? If the first question is always about points... What are we accidentally teaching her to value?
[Q2] "DID YOU WIN?" WHAT YOU SAY: "Did you win?" WHAT YOU MEAN: I hope your team did well. WHAT SHE MAY HEAR: Winning determines whether today was successful. But she doesn't control whether her team wins. She can play incredibly well and lose. She can play terribly and win by 30. She can do everything her coach asks and still lose because another team was simply better. And if winning becomes the measurement for whether the experience was "good"... We're tying her feelings about herself to something she cannot completely control.
[Q3] "DID YOU START?" WHAT YOU SAY: "Did you start?" WHAT YOU MEAN: I'm curious about what happened today. WHAT SHE MAY HEAR: Starting means I'm important. And eventually: Not starting means I'm not good enough. We've already talked about this throughout the course. Starting is a role. Minutes are a role. Neither one determines a player's value as a person. But when those are consistently the first things adults ask about, young athletes notice.
[Q4] "HOW MANY MINUTES DID YOU PLAY?" WHAT YOU SAY: "How much did you play?" WHAT SHE MAY HEAR: My playing time determines whether this was worth it. Now imagine she's already upset about her minutes. She gets into the car. She's processing it. And the first thing she hears is: "Why didn't you play more?" Even if you're frustrated for her, she may begin believing she should be frustrated too.
[Q5] "WHY DID COACH TAKE YOU OUT?" This is another one. WHAT YOU SAY: "Why did Coach take you out?" Maybe you're genuinely curious. Maybe you don't understand the substitution. Maybe you're frustrated because you think your daughter deserved to stay in. WHAT SHE MAY HEAR: Coach did something wrong to me. Or: I should be angry that I came out. Or even: Coach doesn't believe in me. And suddenly something that may have been completely normal becomes emotionally loaded.
[Q6] "YOU SHOULD HAVE SHOT THAT." Or: "Why didn't you shoot?" "You need to score more." "Stop passing so much." "You were wide open." WHAT SHE MAY HEAR: My value comes from producing offensively. And now we've created a player who may start forcing shots because she knows she's going to get into the car afterward and talk about how many points she scored. That's bigger than one comment. That's shaping how she sees the game.
[Q7] "YOU GUYS SHOULD HAVE BEAT THEM." WHAT YOU SAY: "You guys should've won that game." WHAT SHE MAY HEAR: Losing that game was a failure. And now instead of processing what she learned, what she did well, what she needs to improve, or how she competed... She's processing disappointment.
[Q8] "YOU'RE BETTER THAN HER." This one usually comes from love. A parent sees another player getting more minutes, attention, recognition, or recruiting interest. And says: "You're way better than her." WHAT YOU MEAN: I believe in you. WHAT SHE MAY HEAR: Her success takes something away from me. Or: If someone else gets something I want, I should compare myself to them. Or: I deserve what she has. Be careful. Comparison can quietly turn teammates into competition and other girls' success into a threat.
[Q9] "THAT COACH DOESN'T KNOW WHAT THEY'RE DOING." Maybe you believe it. You may even be right. But your daughter hears you. WHAT SHE MAY HEAR: I don't have to respect decisions I disagree with. If I'm not getting what I want, the problem must be the coach. That makes accepting coaching much harder. Because now she's hearing one voice at practice... and another voice in the car.
[F1] THE FIRST QUESTION MATTERS. I really want parents to think about this. Your daughter walks out of the gym. Before you ask anything... WHAT DOES SHE EXPECT YOU TO ASK? That's revealing. Does she already know it's going to be: "How many points?" "Did you win?" "Why didn't you play?" "Why did Coach do that?" Because if she knows... She has already learned what matters to you. Even if you never intended to teach it.
[F2] KIDS LEARN WHAT WE VALUE THROUGH WHAT WE REPEATEDLY NOTICE. Think about it. If after every game we ask about scoring... Scoring must be important. If we always ask about winning... Winning must be important. If we always ask about starting... Starting must be important. If we constantly talk about minutes... Minutes must be important. If we constantly criticize the coach... Coach must be the problem. If we compare her to teammates... Other players must be her competition. Over time, those messages add up.
[F3] AND THEN WE WONDER WHY... She forces shots. She gets devastated after a bad scoring game. She can't enjoy a win when she didn't play well. She can't handle coming off the bench. She watches the scoreboard instead of playing. She's jealous when a teammate succeeds. She thinks every substitution is punishment. She feels like she disappointed you after a bad game. She can't separate basketball performance from how she feels about herself. Sometimes we think those attitudes came out of nowhere. Sometimes... we've been unintentionally reinforcing them for years.
[F4] THIS IS NOT ABOUT BLAMING PARENTS. Most parents are asking these questions because they care. You drive to practices. Pay for tournaments. Buy shoes. Sit in gyms all weekend. Travel. Feed them between games. Rearrange your schedule. Celebrate them. Comfort them. And you want to know what happened. This isn't about making parents afraid to speak after a basketball game. It's about recognizing something incredibly powerful: YOUR WORDS CARRY MORE WEIGHT THAN YOU REALIZE. Especially when they're young. Especially when they care what you think. Especially when basketball matters deeply to them.
[F5] LISTEN TO YOUR OWN LANGUAGE. For now, I don't even want you to change anything. We'll talk about that next. I just want you to notice. For the next few practices and games, listen to yourself. What do you ask first? What do you talk about most? What gets the biggest reaction from you? What do you praise? What frustrates you? What do you talk about on the car ride home? And then ask yourself: "IF I WERE MY DAUGHTER, WHAT WOULD I THINK MATTERS MOST TO MY PARENT?" Not what you intend to communicate. What your repeated words are actually communicating. Because there can be a big difference between: WHAT A PARENT SAYS and WHAT AN ATHLETE HEARS. And before we change the conversation... WE HAVE TO HEAR IT FIRST.
