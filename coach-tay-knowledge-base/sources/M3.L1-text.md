[¶1] WHY ACADEMICS MATTER MORE THAN YOU THINK
[¶2] When families think about recruiting, academics are often treated like something that happens in the background. They shouldn't be.
[¶3] Your daughter's grades can affect whether she is eligible, how much a school can cost, and how attractive she is to a college coach. And that matters at every level.
[¶4] First: Let's talk about the money. The way athletic scholarships work is different depending on the division.
[¶5] At Division I, the scholarship landscape changed significantly beginning in 2025. D1 schools that opt into the new House-settlement model no longer operate under the old sport-by-sport scholarship limits. Instead, they have roster limits, and schools can offer scholarships to athletes on those rosters.
[¶6] That means you should not think about academics at D1 as simply a way to "earn scholarship money instead of basketball money." Basketball is still a major part of the equation. But that doesn't make academics unimportant.
[¶7] At Division II, academics can become an even bigger financial tool. DII basketball operates under a partial-scholarship model. A coach can divide athletic scholarship money among multiple players, and athletic aid can be combined with academic scholarships, grants and other financial assistance.
[¶8] That means a player might have: Basketball money + academic money = a much stronger financial package.
[¶9] At Division III, there are no athletic scholarships. But that does NOT mean DIII is automatically expensive. DIII schools can provide merit-based and need-based financial aid, which means your daughter's academic profile can play a major role in what the family ultimately pays.
[¶10] And the same idea can apply at the NAIA level, where academic aid can be an important part of the overall financial package.
[¶11] So when you're looking at colleges, don't only ask: "How much basketball scholarship money can we get?" Ask: "What is the total financial package this school could put together for my daughter?" That is a much smarter question.
[¶12] But here's the part parents sometimes miss. Academics aren't only about money. College coaches want players they can trust.
[¶13] Think about what happens after your daughter gets to campus. She isn't just going to practice and play games. She's going to class. She's going to take exams. She's going to write papers. She's going to have study hours. She's going to have meetings with academic advisors. And she's going to have to balance all of that with a demanding basketball schedule.
[¶14] College athletics departments pay very close attention to academic performance and eligibility. So coaches don't want to recruit a player who they believe will require constant academic intervention. They want to recruit a player who has already demonstrated that she can handle responsibility.
[¶15] That's why coaches look at more than just the basketball player. They're asking themselves: Can she handle college? Can she manage her time? Does she take school seriously? Will she be eligible? Will I have to constantly chase her about assignments and grades? Will she be responsible enough to manage the demands of being a college athlete? Those questions matter.
[¶16] And this can be a recruiting separator. Imagine a coach is deciding between two players. Maybe they're both good enough. Maybe they're similar positions. Maybe they're similar physically. Maybe both could help the team. But one player has consistently strong grades, takes school seriously and has demonstrated that she can manage her responsibilities. The other player has struggled academically and has shown that school isn't a priority. Those things can absolutely affect the decision.
[¶17] It isn't always going to be the reason one player gets recruited and another doesn't. But when everything else is close, you want every advantage you can have. Your grades may be one of those advantages.
[¶18] Don't wait until recruiting starts to care about academics. This is why academics belong in your recruiting plan from the beginning.
[¶19] Your daughter should know:
- What grades she needs
- What classes she needs
- What her target GPA is
- What core courses are required
- Whether she is on track for NCAA eligibility
- What academic standards the schools she's interested in expect
- What scholarships she may qualify for
- What test scores may strengthen her academic opportunities
[¶20] And remember: "qualifier" is a word you're going to hear throughout the recruiting process. It refers to whether an athlete has met the applicable academic eligibility requirements to compete.
[¶21] But don't make the mistake of thinking: "I just need to be a qualifier." That's the minimum. The goal should be to build an academic profile that gives your daughter options.
[¶22] Because the better her academics are, the more doors she can potentially keep open — athletically, academically and financially.
[¶23] Your daughter is not just being recruited as a basketball player. She's being recruited as a student-athlete. And coaches are evaluating both sides of that equation.
[¶24] Don't just prepare her to play college basketball. Prepare her to be successful in college.
