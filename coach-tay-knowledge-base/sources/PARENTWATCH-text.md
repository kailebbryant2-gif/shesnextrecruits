# PARENTWATCH-T · Coaches Are Watching Parents Too (written text, parents section)

[¶1] Parents, when a college coach walks into the gym to evaluate your daughter... THEY MAY BE WATCHING YOU TOO. Not because they're recruiting you. But because if they recruit your daughter, there's a good chance they're going to have a relationship with your family for the next four years. And coaches know something: THE PLAYER DOESN'T ALWAYS COME ALONE. Sometimes they're recruiting the family dynamic too.
[B1] YOUR BEHAVIOR CAN BECOME PART OF HER EVALUATION. Imagine a coach loves your daughter's game. She's talented. She competes. She communicates. She's coachable. She's exactly what they need. Then they look into the stands and see her parent: Screaming at officials. Arguing every call. Yelling instructions at their daughter. Complaining about substitutions. Making comments about teammates. Confronting coaches. Getting into arguments with other parents. Walking toward the bench after the game angry. Creating a scene. A college coach can absolutely think: "DO I WANT TO DEAL WITH THIS FOR FOUR YEARS?" And that's not a question you want them asking.
[B2] YES — PARENTS CAN HURT RECRUITING. I want to be very clear about this. A parent's behavior can affect how a coach feels about recruiting an athlete. That doesn't mean one frustrated reaction automatically costs your daughter a scholarship. Coaches understand parents are human. Basketball is emotional. Bad calls happen. Games get intense. But there is a difference between being an invested parent... and becoming a liability. If a college coach believes a parent is going to constantly challenge the staff, complain about playing time, create drama, interfere with coaching, or cause problems within the program... That matters. Especially when the coach has other talented players on the recruiting board. Don't give someone an unnecessary reason to move your daughter's name down.
[B3] REMEMBER WHAT YOUR DAUGHTER IS TRYING TO ACCOMPLISH. You might be watching a basketball game. Your daughter might be participating in a job interview. That changes things. At a showcase tournament, live period, elite camp, high school game, or AAU event, you may have no idea who's sitting around you. The woman two rows behind you could be a college coach. The person standing on the baseline could know every coach in your region. Another college staff could be waiting to watch the next game. ACT LIKE SOMEONE IMPORTANT IS ALWAYS WATCHING. Because sometimes they are.
[B4] DON'T COACH FROM THE STANDS. Your daughter already has a coach. Let them coach. Constantly yelling: "SHOOT!" "DRIVE!" "PASS!" "GET BACK!" "WHY ARE YOU DOING THAT?!" creates another voice she has to process while she's trying to play. Even worse, your instruction may contradict what her coach has asked her to do. Now she's deciding: Do I listen to Coach or Mom? Do I run the offense or do what Dad is yelling? Don't put her in that position. BE HER PARENT IN THE STANDS. LET HER COACH BE HER COACH.
[B5] THERE ARE BOUNDARIES WITH YOUR DAUGHTER'S COACH. There are absolutely times when parents need to communicate with a high school or AAU coach. But there is a right way to do it. If you have a legitimate concern: Set up a conversation. Don't confront the coach immediately after an emotional game. Don't corner them walking out of the gym. Don't yell across the court. Don't send an angry text five minutes after the buzzer. And don't make your daughter stand there while you argue about her playing time. Give emotions time to settle. Then communicate respectfully and appropriately.
[B6] KNOW WHAT IS YOUR CONVERSATION — AND WHAT IS HERS. As athletes get older, there are conversations they should begin having themselves. If your daughter doesn't understand her role, encourage her to ask: "Coach, what do I need to improve to earn more opportunities?" If she doesn't understand a correction: She can ask. If she wants to know what the coach needs from her: She can ask. That's part of developing the athlete who will eventually have to communicate with a college coaching staff without Mom or Dad there. Parents can guide her through that conversation. You don't always have to have it for her.
[B7] REFEREES ARE NOT YOUR CONVERSATION. Parents: You are not going to successfully negotiate a call from the stands. Arguing with officials rarely helps your daughter. What it CAN do is show everyone in the gym how your family handles something you believe is unfair. Remember what we told the athletes: Your response matters. That applies to parents too. You can disagree with a call without screaming at an official. You can be frustrated without becoming disrespectful.
[B8] RESPECT EVENT STAFF TOO. This goes beyond coaches and referees. Tournament workers. Scorekeepers. Security. Ticket staff. Hotel employees. Restaurant workers. Other parents. Other players. You don't know who knows who. And more importantly: TREAT PEOPLE WELL EVEN WHEN THEY CAN'T DO ANYTHING FOR YOU. Your daughter is watching that too.
[B9] DON'T TALK BADLY ABOUT OTHER PLAYERS. Be extremely careful with this. "She's not even that good." "I don't know why she starts." "My daughter is way better than her." "Coach only plays her because..." Those conversations don't belong in the stands. First, you never know who's listening. Second, those are someone's kids. And third, you don't want to create an environment where your daughter learns that someone else's success needs to be explained away. Let other girls have their success. Focus on yours.
[B10] BE ESPECIALLY CAREFUL WHEN COLLEGE COACHES ARE PRESENT. If you know a coach is there evaluating your daughter, you do not need to perform for them. You don't need to walk over repeatedly. You don't need to explain your daughter's game. You don't need to tell them why she didn't score more. You don't need to make excuses for an injury, bad game, coach, teammate, referee, or anything else. And you definitely don't need to chase them down after the game asking: "So what did you think?!" Let them evaluate. There will be appropriate opportunities for communication. DON'T FORCE ACCESS.
[B11] FOLLOW THE APPROPRIATE RECRUITING PROTOCOL. As recruiting progresses, there will be times when parents communicate directly with college coaches. That's normal. There are questions families SHOULD ask. There are conversations about academics, finances, visits, scholarships, expectations, and the overall program that absolutely involve parents. But there's a difference between being: INVOLVED and INTRUSIVE. You want a college coach thinking: "This is a supportive family." Not: "This family is going to run our program from the stands."
[B12] YOUR DAUGHTER IS WATCHING YOU TOO. This might be even more important than the college coach. If you scream at referees... then tell her to control her emotions... What did you teach? If you disrespect her coach... then tell her to be coachable... What did you teach? If you complain about teammates... then tell her to be a good teammate... What did you teach? If you lose control every time something doesn't go her way... then tell her to have a next-play mentality... WHAT DID YOU TEACH? She's learning how to respond to basketball by watching you respond to basketball.
[B13] YOU DON'T HAVE TO BE PERFECT. You can cheer. You can get excited. You can be disappointed. You can disagree. You can celebrate loudly. You can love watching your daughter compete. You don't need to sit in the stands terrified that you're going to do something wrong. Just remember the boundary. SUPPORT HER. DON'T BECOME THE STORY. Because when a college coach leaves the gym, you want them talking about your daughter. Her game. Her effort. Her toughness. Her communication. Her potential. Not: "Did you see her parents?"
[B14] DON'T LET YOUR BEHAVIOR CREATE A QUESTION MARK NEXT TO HER NAME. Your daughter is doing everything she can to become recruitable. She's practicing. Lifting. Traveling. Watching film. Going to camps. Sending emails. Building relationships. Trying to get her name onto a recruiting board. Parents, your role is to help her. Not accidentally make that job harder. BE SUPPORTIVE. BE RESPECTFUL. KNOW THE BOUNDARIES. LET COACHES COACH. LET OFFICIALS OFFICIATE. LET YOUR DAUGHTER COMPETE. And when a college coach happens to look into the stands... MAKE SURE THEY SEE A FAMILY THEY'D BE HAPPY TO WELCOME INTO THEIR PROGRAM.
