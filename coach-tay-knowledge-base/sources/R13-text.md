[¶1] Elite camps can be a great tool in the recruiting process, but I want you to understand what they are — and what they aren't.
[¶2] An invitation to an elite camp does not automatically mean a college coach is ready to offer your daughter a scholarship. In many cases, these camps are primarily about exposure, evaluation, and getting your daughter into an environment where she can compete against other high-level players. And that can be extremely valuable.
[¶3] But parents need to understand that college programs can also generate revenue from camps. So when your daughter receives an invitation, don't automatically assume: "They invited her, so they must really want her." Sometimes they do genuinely see potential in her. Sometimes the invitation is part of a broader camp marketing strategy. Your job is to learn the difference.
[¶4] So how do you know if the invitation is genuine? Look at who invited her, why she was invited, and what the relationship with the program actually is. Ask yourself: Has a coach from the program actually watched her play? Has someone from the coaching staff communicated with her? Is the invitation personalized or does it feel like a mass invitation? Is the camp appropriate for her current ability and recruiting level? Does the school genuinely fit what your daughter is looking for? Is there a reason this particular camp makes sense for her recruiting plan?
[¶5] An invitation can be encouraging, but don't confuse an invitation with recruiting interest.
[¶6] Elite camps can still be incredibly valuable. Your daughter gets to compete against players she may not normally see. She gets to experience a college gym and college coaching environment. She gets to see what players at different levels look like. And most importantly, she gets a chance to build confidence. Sometimes a player doesn't realize how good she is until she walks into a gym full of talented players and realizes: "I belong here." That experience can be just as valuable as the recruiting exposure itself.
[¶7] Freshman Year: Experience. Freshman year is a great time to start attending elite camps with the goal of learning and gaining experience. Let her experience different college environments. Let her compete against new players. Let her learn what college coaches are looking for. And pay attention to how she responds. Does she rise to the competition? Does she communicate? Does she compete when she's not the best player in the gym? Does she handle being challenged? These experiences can help her understand where she fits and what she needs to continue developing.
[¶8] Sophomore Year: Be More Intentional. By sophomore year, I want the purpose of attending camps to become much more specific. Instead of simply asking: "What elite camps can we go to?" Start asking: "What schools and programs are actually interesting to my daughter?" If she is interested in a particular school, attending that school's camp can be a great opportunity to get in front of that coaching staff, learn more about the program, and see whether the school is actually a good fit. This is where you should begin moving from general exposure to intentional exposure. You don't need to attend every camp you're invited to. You need to attend the camps that make sense for her development, her recruiting level, and her interests.
[¶9] Remember: An invitation is not an offer. A camp is not a guarantee of recruiting interest. But the right camp, at the right time, can give your daughter valuable exposure, confidence, experience, and information about where she fits. Be intentional with your money. Be intentional with your time. And most importantly, know WHY you're attending.
