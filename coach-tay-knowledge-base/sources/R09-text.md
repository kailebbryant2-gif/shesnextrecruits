[¶1] Now we get to the 2 and 3 guard spots. And this is where recruiting gets much more competitive.
[¶2] THIS IS THE BIGGEST POOL. There are simply more players who fit this description. There are a lot of girls who are: Good shooters; Good athletes; Good defenders; Good ball handlers; Good scorers; Good high school players. And because there are so many of them, coaches know they have options. Remember the recruiting board? A coach may need one or two guards for a class, but there are going to be a lot of players who could potentially fill those spots. That means coaches can afford to be more selective. And because of that, the typical 2/3 guard is often recruited later than the harder-to-find positions. That doesn't mean your daughter can't play college basketball. It doesn't mean she shouldn't play the 2 or 3. It simply means: If you're in the biggest pool, you need something that separates you from the pool.
[¶3] WHAT MAKES HER DIFFERENT? If your daughter is not: One of the most skilled players in the pool or One of the taller players in the pool then I want you to start thinking differently. Ask: What is her superpower? What is the thing she does that makes a coach say: "I need to have this kid." Maybe it's strength. If your daughter is naturally strong and physical, maybe she should develop into a bigger, more versatile player who can give a coach minutes at the 4. Maybe it's speed. If she's incredibly fast, maybe she should develop her point guard skills so she can pressure the ball, handle pressure, push the pace and run a team. Maybe it's defense. Maybe she's the player who can lock down the other team's best guard. Maybe it's rebounding. Maybe she is relentless on the glass despite being a guard. Maybe it's her ability to attack the basket. Maybe she's an incredible passer. Maybe she sees the floor differently than everyone else. Find the superpower — and then build the rest of her game around it.
[¶4] BEING A GREAT SHOOTER ISN'T ALWAYS ENOUGH. And this one is personal for me. I was a phenomenal 3-point shooter. That was one of my biggest strengths. And it helped me get recruited. But there was a problem. What happened when my shot wasn't falling? Because there are going to be days when your shot doesn't fall. There are going to be games when you go 1-for-8. There are going to be stretches where you're not making shots in practice. There are going to be nights when every player on the floor is struggling to put the ball in the basket. And if shooting is the only thing you bring to the floor... you have a problem. I learned that very quickly in college.
[¶5] THE CONVERSATION I NEVER FORGOT. During preseason, my college coach basically gave me a wake-up call. He told me: "If you're not making shots, you're pretty much worthless to me." It was a kick in the butt. And it wasn't because he thought I was a bad player. It was because he was telling me something I needed to understand: My minutes couldn't depend entirely on whether or not my shot was falling. If I wanted to stay on the floor, I had to figure out how to be productive even when I wasn't scoring. Could I defend? Could I rebound? Could I get on the floor? Could I make the extra pass? Could I take care of the ball? Could I communicate? Could I make the right decision? Could I make my teammates better? Could I bring energy? Could I do the little things that help a team win? That's when I really understood the value of being an all-around player.
[¶6] THIS IS WHY I KEEP TALKING ABOUT INTANGIBLES. If you haven't gotten to the Intangibles section yet, please take that module seriously. I mean that. The things that don't necessarily show up in the box score can absolutely help separate your daughter from the crowd. Her communication. Her toughness. Her competitiveness. Her response to mistakes. Her coachability. Her effort. Her ability to make teammates better. Her willingness to do things that don't get attention. Those things matter. And sometimes those things are what get a player recruited in the first place. But once you're on a college roster, you still have to find ways to contribute.
[¶7] YOU NEED A FALLBACK. This is what I want you to think about. If your daughter's superpower is shooting, great. But what happens when the shot isn't falling? If her superpower is speed, great. But what happens when she can't get downhill? If her superpower is scoring, great. But what happens when someone is defending her? If her superpower is size, great. But what happens when she has to defend someone smaller and faster? A college coach wants to know: "What else can you give me?" That's where versatility comes in. You don't need to make your daughter an expert at everything. But you should develop her into the best all-around player her skill set will allow her to become.
[¶8] YOU WANT TO STAND OUT FROM THE CROWD. If there are 100 guards trying to get the same spot, being: "A really good shooter" may not be enough. But being: "A really good shooter who can defend the other team's best guard..." Now you're different. "A really good shooter who can also handle point..." Different. "A really good shooter who is incredibly physical and rebounds..." Different. "A really good shooter who never makes the same mistake twice and makes everyone around her better..." Different. That's how you start separating yourself. DON'T JUST BE GOOD AT THE THING EVERYONE ELSE IS GOOD AT. Figure out what makes your daughter hard to replace.
[¶9] THE BIG TAKEAWAY. If your daughter is a typical 2/3 guard, she's competing in the largest pool of players. That doesn't mean she can't get recruited. It means she needs to think strategically about what makes her different. Find her superpower. Develop it. Then build the rest of her game so she can still contribute when that superpower isn't working. Because college basketball is not: "Can you do one thing really well?" It's: "Can you help me win?" And the more ways your daughter can answer yes to that question, the more valuable she becomes. Don't just train to be a great 2 or 3. Train to be the player a coach can't afford to leave off the floor.
