# STRENGTH-T · Why Strength Training Matters to College Coaches (written text)

[¶1] When a high school recruit arrives at college, she may be 18 years old playing against women who are 19, 20, 21, or 22. Some of those players have already spent years in college strength programs, lifting consistently, conditioning, learning how to recover, and developing their bodies for the demands of college basketball. There will naturally be an adjustment to the speed, physicality, and demands of the college game. The goal is to help your daughter arrive at college prepared for that adjustment — not starting from zero.
[¶2] Strength Training Is Another Way to Become MORE MARKETABLE. If two players have similar skill, size, and athleticism, but one has taken strength training seriously and has developed a stronger, more physical body, that player has an advantage. That's not because she can necessarily lift more weight. It's because strength shows up on the basketball court.
[¶3] College coaches care about whether a player can: Handle contact; Finish through contact; Hold her position; Defend stronger players; Rebound through traffic; Fight through screens; Stay strong while moving; Maintain her physicality late in games; Handle the demands of a college practice and season. Those things matter.
[¶4] Strength Isn't About the Number on the Bar. I don't want you to look at strength training and think: "How much can she bench?" That's not the point. The point is: What can she do on the basketball court because she is stronger? Can she absorb contact without getting knocked off balance? Can she hold her defensive position? Can she finish when someone is bumping her? Can she battle for a rebound? Can she maintain her movement and physicality when she's tired? That's what strength training should ultimately help create.
[¶5] Start Learning Before College. Younger athletes don't need to train like college athletes. They need to learn how to move well, control their bodies, develop coordination, understand technique, and gradually build strength. As athletes get older, training can become more structured and progressively challenging. The goal isn't to maximize strength as quickly as possible. The goal is safe, consistent, age-appropriate development.
[¶6] By the time your daughter gets to college, she shouldn't be completely unfamiliar with what it means to train her body. She will still learn her college program from the strength coach. She will still have plenty to improve. But she shouldn't be walking into the weight room thinking: "I've never done this before."
[¶7] Don't Wait Until College to Start Learning How to Be a College Athlete. College basketball is a huge physical adjustment. The more prepared your daughter is for that adjustment, the better. And remember — recruiting is about more than what shows up in the scoring column. When two players are similar, the separators matter. Strength can be a separator. Physicality can be a separator. Training habits can be a separator. Recovery can be a separator. Don't just become a more skilled basketball player. Become a more complete athlete.
