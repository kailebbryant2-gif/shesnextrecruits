[¶1] Your AAU coach and high school coach can be some of the most valuable people in your daughter's recruiting process. But I want to clear up a common misconception: They do not have to have a huge list of college connections to help your daughter.
[¶2] In the beginning, their most important role is often to be the middleman between your family and college coaches. They can help college coaches get an honest, outside perspective on your daughter — and that can be incredibly valuable.
[¶3] But here's the reality... Most coaches are not recruiting coordinators. They may not know exactly how to contact college coaches. They may not know what information to send. They may not know which coaches to contact. And many of them have full-time jobs, families, and their own responsibilities outside of basketball. So don't assume: "My coach will take care of recruiting." They probably won't — and that's not necessarily because they don't care. Sometimes they simply don't know what to do.
[¶4] Make it easy for them to help you. Later in this course, we're going to teach you how to build your list of schools and how to find the appropriate coaching staff contact information. Once you have that information, you can give your coach something incredibly useful: A specific list and a specific plan.
[¶5] Instead of saying: "Coach, can you help get my daughter recruited?" You can say: "Here are the schools we're interested in. Here are the coaches' email addresses. Here's the information about my daughter. And here's a draft email you can use or modify when reaching out on her behalf." Now you've taken the guesswork out of it. Your coach doesn't have to spend hours researching schools or figuring out who to contact. You've done the work. You're simply giving them the tools to help.
[¶6] What can your coach actually do? Your AAU or high school coach can: Reach out to college coaches on your daughter's behalf; Introduce your daughter to a coaching staff; Give an honest evaluation of her abilities; Explain her strengths and areas of development; Help coaches understand her role on the team; Help coaches find her at tournaments or games; Take calls from college coaches; Answer questions about her character and work ethic; Help communicate your family's level of interest; Give coaches an outside perspective on whether your daughter may be a good fit.
[¶7] And that last part can be especially valuable. Coaches want honesty. College coaches know that families want options. They also know that sometimes a family will tell a school: "She's very interested!" when they are actually considering ten other schools and haven't really decided what they want. Your AAU or high school coach can sometimes provide a more objective perspective.
[¶8] A college coach may call your daughter's coach and ask: "How serious are they about our program?" "Do you think we'd be a good fit?" "Is she actually interested in us?" "Where does she stand compared to other schools she's talking to?" Your coach can give them information that you, as the parent, simply can't provide in the same way. That's why having a coach who knows your daughter's goals and understands where she stands with different programs can be extremely helpful.
[¶9] Keep your coaches informed. Your coaches should not have to guess what is happening with your daughter's recruiting. If a school reaches out, let them know. If your daughter becomes interested in a school, let them know. If she is no longer interested, let them know. If a school invites her to a camp, let them know. If she has a great conversation with a coaching staff, let them know. You don't need to give your coach every tiny detail, but open communication matters. Your coach can only advocate for your daughter effectively if they understand what you're trying to accomplish.
[¶10] Your coach is part of the team — not the entire team. Remember what we talked about earlier: Your recruiting team should stay small and organized. Your daughter has a role. You have a role. Your AAU coach has a role. Your high school coach has a role. And everyone should understand what that role is.
[¶11] Don't expect your coaches to do everything for you. But don't leave their help sitting on the table either. Give them the information. Give them the tools. Give them clear direction. Then let them use their position as coaches to help advocate for your daughter. You may be surprised by how far a simple, honest conversation between a college coach and a high school or AAU coach can go.
[¶12] Your coaches don't need to have all the connections. They need to know how to help — and you need to make it easy for them to do it.
