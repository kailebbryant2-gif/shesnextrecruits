[¶1] If your daughter wants to play NCAA basketball, you are going to hear the words NCAA Eligibility Center a lot. Don't let it intimidate you.
[¶2] The Eligibility Center is essentially the NCAA's way of determining whether a prospective student-athlete has met the requirements to compete at an NCAA school. It looks at two major areas: Academic eligibility — her courses, grades, core-course GPA, transcripts and graduation. Athletics eligibility — her sports participation history and other activities that may be relevant under NCAA rules.
[¶3] For Division I and Division II athletes, the Eligibility Center handles the certification process.
[¶4] And this is important: Being recruited does not automatically mean you're eligible. A coach can want your daughter very badly, but she still has to meet the NCAA's eligibility requirements.

[S1] STEP 1: CREATE THE RIGHT ACCOUNT. The NCAA currently has different types of accounts, so don't just create an account without understanding which one your daughter needs. If she is planning to compete at a Division I or Division II school, she will generally need an Academic and Athletics Certification Account. If she's simply exploring her options and isn't ready for certification yet, she can start with a free Profile Page Account and transition to a certification account later. And yes — walk-ons and preferred walk-ons still have to meet NCAA initial-eligibility requirements. Don't assume that because your daughter isn't receiving a scholarship, the rules don't apply to her. One important tip: Use an email address your daughter will continue to have access to after high school. This account can follow her through the recruiting process, so don't use an email address that belongs to a parent, AAU program or school if she may lose access to it later.

[S2] STEP 2: ENTER HER EDUCATION HISTORY. When you register, the NCAA wants to know where your daughter has gone to school. That means you need to list the high schools, secondary schools and applicable educational programs she has attended. This is important even if she didn't receive grades or credits from one of those programs. If she has transferred schools, attended multiple high schools, taken courses through another program or has an unusual academic history, don't assume the NCAA already has that information. Make sure her education history is accurate.

[S3] STEP 3: MAKE SURE HER COURSES ARE NCAA-APPROVED. This is one of the biggest things I want parents to understand. Not every high school class counts toward NCAA eligibility. Your daughter can have a great overall GPA and still have a problem if she hasn't completed the required NCAA-approved core courses. This is why you need to get her high school counselor involved early. Her counselor can help you look at the school's NCAA-approved course list and make sure the classes she is taking will count toward eligibility. Don't wait until senior year to discover that she took the wrong classes. This is one of the reasons academics need to be part of recruiting from the beginning.

[S4] STEP 4: UNDERSTAND THE CORE-COURSE REQUIREMENT. For Division I and Division II, your daughter needs 16 NCAA-approved core courses. The requirements are different between the two divisions.
Division I requires: 4 years English; 3 years math, including Algebra I or higher; 2 years science; 1 additional year of English, math or science; 2 years social science; 4 additional core courses.
Division I also has a 10/7 requirement: 10 of the 16 core courses must be completed before the beginning of the seventh semester, and 7 of those must be in English, math or science.
Division I currently requires a minimum 2.3 NCAA core-course GPA.
Division II also requires 16 NCAA-approved core courses, but the distribution is different: 3 years English; 2 years math, including Algebra I or higher; 2 years science; 3 additional years of English, math or science; 2 years social science; 4 additional core courses.
Division II currently requires a minimum 2.2 NCAA core-course GPA.
Notice that I said NCAA core-course GPA, not her regular high school GPA. Those are not necessarily the same number.

[S5] STEP 5: SEND HER TRANSCRIPTS. The NCAA needs her academic records. Her high school counselor or designated school official submits official transcripts to the Eligibility Center. If she has attended multiple high schools, make sure each school submits the required official records. Don't assume the newest high school automatically has everything the NCAA needs. Missing transcripts or other required documents can delay certification.

[S6] STEP 6: COMPLETE THE SPORTS INFORMATION. The Eligibility Center doesn't only look at academics. Your daughter will also provide information about her athletic participation. This can include information about: Teams she has played for; Events and tournaments; Athletic expenses; Awards; People who have advised her; People or organizations that have marketed her athletic ability. The NCAA can also add additional questions or tasks if it needs more information. This is another reason I want you keeping good records throughout the recruiting process. Know what teams she's played for. Know what events she's attended. Know who has been involved in her basketball development. Don't wait until senior year and try to reconstruct everything from memory.

[S7] STEP 7: PAY THE REGISTRATION FEE OR REQUEST A FEE WAIVER. There is a registration fee for certification accounts, although eligible students may be able to receive a fee waiver. The NCAA's current registration page should always be your source for the current fee because fees can change. Once the registration is completed, keep an eye on the account. Registering is not the same thing as being certified. That's an important distinction.

[S8] STEP 8: WATCH HER NCAA ACCOUNT. This is where I want parents and athletes to get into a habit. Log into the account. Look at the task list. See whether the NCAA is asking for anything. Check whether transcripts have been received. Make sure her education history and sports information are accurate. The Eligibility Center uses tasks to tell you when additional information is needed. Don't create the account freshman year and never look at it again. Her academic situation is changing every year.

[S9] STEP 9: REQUEST FINAL CERTIFICATION. This happens later. During her senior year, your daughter will need to request her final athletics certification before enrolling at her Division I or Division II school. Her final official transcript and proof of graduation also need to be submitted. The NCAA then completes the certification process. So think of this as a process — not one form you fill out once.

[N] WHAT SHOULD PARENTS DO RIGHT NOW? If your daughter is still early in high school, you don't need to panic about getting every piece finished tomorrow. Instead:
[N1] Know which division(s) she's considering. If Division I or II is a possibility, understand the Eligibility Center requirements now.
[N2] Talk to her counselor. Tell the counselor: "My daughter is planning to play college basketball. Can we make sure her schedule is on track for NCAA eligibility?"
[N3] Check the school's NCAA-approved core-course list. Don't assume every class counts.
[N4] Keep her grades up. Remember, her NCAA core-course GPA can be different from the GPA you see on her report card.
[N5] Keep records. Especially if she attends multiple schools, takes courses outside her normal school or has an unusual academic history.
[N6] Don't wait until senior year. The earlier you understand the requirements, the easier it is to fix a problem.

[D3] ONE MORE THING: DIVISION III. If your daughter is considering Division III, don't automatically assume she needs the same certification process. Domestic Division III athletes generally do not go through NCAA academic certification in the same way Division I and II athletes do. Division III schools establish their own academic standards on campus. The Eligibility Center does handle athletics certification for Division III international student-athletes. So once your daughter starts narrowing down schools, always look at the requirements for the actual division and school she's considering.

[T] THE BIG TAKEAWAY. The NCAA Eligibility Center is not something you should be afraid of. But it is something you should pay attention to. Your job isn't to become an NCAA compliance expert. Your job is to make sure you're not accidentally putting your daughter in a position where she becomes a great basketball recruit who isn't academically eligible to play. Don't wait until senior year to find out she took the wrong classes. Get informed early. Talk to the counselor. Track the requirements. Keep the account updated. And give your daughter as many options as possible when recruiting gets serious.
