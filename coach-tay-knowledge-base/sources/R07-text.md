[¶1] So we've established that post players tend to get recruited first because height is rare. The next position that I would put high on that recruiting board? Point guards. And there's a reason.
[¶2] POINT GUARD IS A HARD POSITION TO FILL. Being a point guard is about so much more than bringing the ball up the floor. A great point guard has a huge responsibility on the court. She has to: Handle the basketball; Handle pressure; Break pressure; See the floor; Make decisions quickly; Know the offense; Know where every position is supposed to be; Understand what the other four players are supposed to do; Communicate constantly; Lead; Be comfortable having the ball in her hands; Make teammates better; Take criticism from her coach; Give constructive criticism to her teammates; Make adjustments when things aren't working; Be willing to do the uncomfortable parts of the game; Defend; Make the right play instead of always making the flashy play. And that's just the beginning.
[¶3] WHY DO COACHES PUT SO MUCH PRESSURE ON THE POINT GUARD? Because the point guard is often an extension of the coach on the floor. Think about that. When the coach is on the sideline, she can't physically be on the court making every decision. The point guard has to help carry that responsibility. If the coach says: "We're changing what we're doing." The point guard has to understand it. If the other team starts pressuring the ball, she has to handle it. If a teammate is in the wrong spot, she has to recognize it. If the offense isn't working, she has to make a decision. If the game gets uncomfortable, she can't disappear. That's a lot to ask of one player. And that's exactly why good point guards are so valuable.
[¶4] THIS IS WHY I WANT MORE GIRLS TRAINING TO BE POINT GUARDS. If your daughter is a smaller guard — maybe she's a "2" or a "3" right now — I want you to seriously consider this: TRAIN TO BE A POINT GUARD. Or, at the very least: TRAIN TO BE CAPABLE OF PLAYING POINT GUARD. You don't have to completely change who she is. You don't have to tell her she can never play the 2 or the 3. But if she can legitimately give a college coach minutes at the point? You have just made her more marketable. And this is something I wish I understood when I was your daughter's age.
[¶5] WHAT I WISH I HAD KNOWN. I was 5'4". I was not going to walk into a college gym and have coaches recruit me because I was tall. I needed to give a coach another reason to want me. And looking back, I wish I had made myself a much more complete point guard. I wish I had focused even more on: Ball handling. Pressure handling. Decision making. Seeing the floor. Leadership. Communication. Running a team. Because at 5'4", being a really good guard wasn't enough by itself. I needed to become the best possible point guard I could be. That would have made me a much more marketable player. And I want your daughter to understand this before she gets to the point where she's trying to find a college roster spot.
[¶6] GUARDS DON'T ALWAYS WANT TO BE POINT GUARDS. This is the same problem we talked about with tall players who don't want to play post. A lot of guards don't want to play point. They want to be the 2. They want to score. They don't necessarily want to bring the ball up against pressure. They don't want to have the coach yelling at them because they missed a play. They don't want to be responsible for getting everyone organized. They don't want to have the ball when the game gets tight. And honestly? I understand it. Being the point guard can be uncomfortable. But that's exactly why you should develop it. Because if your daughter is a guard who can score and can legitimately play point guard, she has given a coach another option. And we've already talked about how much coaches love options.
[¶7] YOU DON'T HAVE TO BE "THE POINT GUARD". Here's the important distinction. I'm not saying your daughter has to become a traditional, pass-first, full-time point guard. I'm saying she should become capable. If she's a shooting guard who can also bring the ball up and initiate the offense, that's valuable. If she's a wing who can handle pressure and make decisions, that's valuable. If she's a bigger guard who can defend multiple positions and run some point, that's valuable. You don't have to own the position to be valuable at the position. Sometimes being able to give a coach 10 minutes at point guard when they need it can be the difference between being useful and being extremely useful.
[¶8] THINK BACK TO THE RECRUITING BOARD. Remember how we talked about coaches building their recruiting boards? They're not just asking: "Is she a good player?" They're asking: "What do I need?" Imagine two guards who are otherwise very similar. Guard A can only play the 2. Guard B can play the 2 and is capable of playing point. If the coach needs a point guard, who gives that coach more options? Guard B. That's the entire point. You are trying to give a coach more reasons to put your name on that board.
[¶9] THE BIG TAKEAWAY. Point guards are recruited quickly because they are hard to find. There are plenty of girls who can score. There are plenty of girls who can shoot. There are plenty of girls who can play the 2 or the 3. But finding a player who can handle pressure, run a team, see the floor, communicate, lead, make decisions and execute what the coach wants? That's much harder. So if your daughter is a smaller guard, don't automatically say: "She's a 2." Ask: "Could she become capable of playing the point?" Because if she can... YOU JUST MADE HER MORE MARKETABLE. And if there's one thing I wish I had understood when I was 5'4" and playing this game, this is it. I should have worked harder to become the best point guard I could possibly be. I would have given coaches another reason to recruit me. Don't just train for the position you play today. Train for the position that gives you the most opportunities tomorrow.
