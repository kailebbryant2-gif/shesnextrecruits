# MINDSET-T · The Player Within the Player (written text)

- Kept verbatim. Paragraph labels added for citation. Mental-wellbeing content. Not clinical advice.

[¶1] We've spent a lot of time talking about the player everyone else can see. Her shot. Her defense. Her hustle. Her communication. Her body language. Her effort. But there's another player that matters just as much. The player inside her own head. What does she say to herself? How does she respond when she fails? How does she handle pressure? What happens mentally when she misses three shots in a row? What does she believe about herself when she isn't playing well? Can she enjoy basketball while still wanting desperately to be great at it? Because developing the athlete's mind is part of developing the athlete.
[¶2] YOU WANT HER TO CARE. I absolutely want athletes to care. I want your daughter to want to be great. I want her to have goals. I want her to practice when nobody makes her. I want her to be disappointed when she knows she didn't give her best. I want her to want to improve. I want her to dream about playing college basketball if that's what she wants. Caring is a GOOD thing. We don't want to remove the competitive fire from an athlete. But there is a healthy difference between: "Basketball is something I love." and "Basketball determines how I feel about myself." Those are not the same thing.
[¶3] BASKETBALL IS WHAT YOU DO. IT IS NOT WHO YOU ARE. This is something I want athletes to understand as early as possible. You are a basketball player. But you are also a daughter. A sister. A friend. A student. A teammate. A person with interests, relationships, gifts, and a future that extends far beyond basketball. Basketball can be an enormous part of your life. It shouldn't have to carry the weight of your entire identity. Because if your entire identity is: "I am good at basketball." What happens when you play badly? What happens when you aren't starting? What happens when you get injured? What happens when another player gets recruited before you? What happens when you miss the game-winning shot? Suddenly, a basketball problem can start feeling like a you problem. Instead of: "I had a bad game." It becomes: "I'm bad." Instead of: "I made a mistake." It becomes: "I'm a failure." Those are very different statements.
[¶4] YOU ARE ALLOWED TO WANT TO BE GREAT WITHOUT BEING PERFECT. This might be one of the most important things in this entire section. Great players make mistakes. Great players miss shots. Great players turn the ball over. Great players get beat defensively. Great players have terrible practices. Great players have games where nothing seems to work. Great players get corrected. Great players sometimes lose. You do not need perfection to become great. In fact, if your expectation is perfection, basketball is going to be miserable. Because basketball is a game of mistakes. The question isn't: "Can I avoid ever messing up?" The question is: "How quickly can I move forward when I do?"
[¶5] PAY ATTENTION TO HOW YOU TALK TO YOURSELF. Imagine your teammate misses a shot. Would you run over and say: "You're terrible." "You always mess everything up." "You can't shoot." "You're embarrassing yourself." Hopefully not. So why would you talk to yourself that way? Athletes can become their own worst critic. And sometimes they think being extremely hard on themselves means they care more. It doesn't. You can hold yourself accountable without tearing yourself apart. There is a difference between: "That wasn't good enough. I need to fix it." and "I'm not good enough." One identifies a behavior. The other attacks your identity. Learn the difference.
[¶6] CHANGE THE CONVERSATION. When something goes wrong, practice changing your internal response. Instead of: "I can't do this." Try: "Next play." Instead of: "I'm playing terrible." Try: "What can I control right now?" Instead of: "I can't make anything." Try: "Keep taking good shots." Instead of: "Coach is mad at me." Try: "What is coach asking me to fix?" Instead of: "I just ruined everything." Try: "Sprint back and make the next play." Notice something? We're not pretending the mistake didn't happen. We're not saying: "Everything is amazing!" We're giving our brain something productive to do next. That's healthy self-talk.
[¶7] TALK TO YOURSELF LIKE A COACH. I love this idea for athletes: Become your own coach. A good coach doesn't watch you make one mistake and decide your entire career is over. They identify the problem. They give you information. And then they want you to make the next play. Learn to do that for yourself. Mistake. Recognize it. Correction. What should I have done? Response. Do it better next time. Then... MOVE ON. Don't spend the next four possessions mentally replaying something you cannot change.
[¶8] THE NEXT-PLAY MENTALITY. Basketball moves too fast for you to live in the last possession. You miss a layup. Next play. Turn it over. Next play. Get beat. Next play. Bad call. Next play. Coach takes you out. Next play. Your brain is going to want to stay behind and think about what just happened. Train it to come back to right now. Ask yourself: "What does my team need from me NEXT?" That question can completely change the way you respond to mistakes.
[¶9] MOTIVATION IS STRONGEST WHEN SOME OF IT COMES FROM HER. Parents and coaches can encourage an athlete. They can provide opportunities. They can drive her to workouts. They can help create structure. They can hold standards. But eventually, she has to want some of this for herself. The athlete who develops her own reasons for working tends to have something powerful: Ownership. She practices because she wants to improve. She watches film because she wants to understand. She lifts because she understands why it matters. She gets shots up because she has goals. She communicates with coaches because she wants to play college basketball. That's very different from an athlete who is constantly being dragged through the process by everyone around her. You cannot manufacture someone's dream for them. At some point, the athlete has to begin saying: "This is what I want."
[¶10] BUT MOTIVATION WILL NOT FEEL THE SAME EVERY DAY. Being self-motivated doesn't mean waking up every morning excited to work. Nobody feels that way all the time. There will be days when you're tired. Days when practice doesn't sound fun. Days when you're frustrated. Days when your confidence is low. Days when basketball feels hard. That's normal. This is where discipline and habits become important. Motivation might get you started. Your habits help you continue.
[¶11] DON'T LET ONE GAME DECIDE HOW YOU FEEL ABOUT YOURSELF. This is something I really want athletes to work on. You cannot ride the emotional roller coaster of: Great game: "I'm amazing." Bad game: "I'm terrible." Coach calls: "I'm going Division I!" Coach doesn't respond: "Nobody wants me." Start: "I'm good." Come off the bench: "I'm not good enough." That is exhausting. Your confidence needs deeper roots than the result of your last game. You should be able to say: "I didn't play well today AND I still believe in the player I'm becoming." Both things can be true.
[¶12] CONFIDENCE IS NOT THINKING YOU'RE PERFECT. Confidence isn't: "I'm the best player here." Confidence can be: "I'm prepared." "I trust the work I've done." "I can make a mistake and recover." "I can handle being coached." "I don't have to prove myself on every possession." "I belong here." That's a much more sustainable kind of confidence.
[¶13] SEPARATE YOUR PERFORMANCE FROM YOUR WORTH. I want athletes to really hear this: Your performance can be evaluated. That's sports. There are going to be times when someone says: "You didn't play well." "Your defense wasn't good enough." "You're turning the ball over too much." "Your shot selection needs to improve." "You're not ready for that level yet." Those conversations aren't always comfortable. But feedback about your basketball performance is not a statement about your worth as a person. You can say: "I need to get better." without believing: "I'm not enough." That's a very important distinction.
[¶14] A HEALTHY ATHLETE CAN HOLD TWO THINGS AT ONCE. I LOVE basketball. AND There is more to me than basketball. I want to be great. AND I don't have to be perfect. I'm proud of myself. AND I have things I need to improve. I'm disappointed in how I played. AND I'm still okay. I believe in myself. AND I can accept coaching. Those things don't contradict each other. That's maturity.
[¶15] PROTECT THE PLAYER WITHIN THE PLAYER. Your basketball skills matter. Your body matters. Your strength matters. Your film matters. Your recruiting strategy matters. But the person carrying all of those things matters too. Take care of her. Develop her. Teach her how to respond to failure. Teach her how to talk to herself. Teach her that mistakes are information, not identity. Teach her how to compete without being consumed by competition. Teach her to dream big without believing she has to be perfect. And help her eventually take ownership of why she's doing all of this. Because we aren't trying to create an athlete who only performs well when everything is going right. We're trying to develop an athlete who knows how to handle herself when it isn't.
[¶16] THE PLAYER EVERYONE SEES STARTS WITH THE PLAYER NOBODY CAN SEE. Her thoughts. Her confidence. Her response. Her motivation. Her identity. Her ability to move forward. Develop that player, too.
