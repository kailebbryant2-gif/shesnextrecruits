[¶1] One of the biggest mistakes I see with young basketball players is deciding what position they are too early. She's tall, so she must be a post. She's smaller, so she must be a point guard. She's athletic, so let's make her a wing. Stop. Your daughter is a developing basketball player — not a finished product.
[¶2] THE "SHE'S TALL, MAKE HER A POST" PROBLEM. This happens all the time. A young girl is taller than everyone else, so she gets put in the post. She spends years playing inside. She isn't asked to handle the ball. She isn't asked to bring it up the floor. She isn't encouraged to shoot outside. She isn't taught how to attack from the perimeter. Then she stops growing. Suddenly, she's a 5'10" or 6'0" player who has spent years being developed like a traditional post — and now she's trying to learn guard and perimeter skills that other players have been developing for years.
[¶3] Being tall is an advantage. It should not become a limitation. And the same thing happens in the opposite direction. Just because your daughter is smaller does not mean she should ignore everything that happens inside the lane.
[¶4] DEVELOP THE COMPLETE PLAYER FIRST. I strongly recommend that young players work on all areas of the game through at least freshman year — and even beyond that, development should remain much broader than most athletes realize. That means your daughter should become familiar with: Ball handling; Layups and finishing; Finishing through contact; Mid-range shooting; 3-point shooting; Shooting off the dribble; Passing; Attacking closeouts; Attacking from different areas of the floor; Playing off screens; Creating shots; Rebounding; Perimeter defense; Post defense; Post moves; Playing with and without the ball; Reading the game; Making decisions.
[¶5] Does that mean your daughter will eventually be equally good at all of those things? No. It means she should have exposure to all of them before deciding what her game will ultimately become. Because you don't know exactly how tall she's going to be. You don't know how her body is going to develop. You don't know what position she'll play in college. And you certainly don't know what a future college coach will need her to do.
[¶6] A GREAT PLAYER KNOWS THE WHOLE GAME. Here's what I want you to understand: Position is not the same thing as skill set. A post player who can handle the ball, pass, shoot and attack from the perimeter is incredibly valuable. A guard who can rebound, finish through contact, defend bigger players and make plays around the basket is incredibly valuable. A wing who can do both has even more options. The more areas of the game your daughter understands, the more ways she can help a team.
[¶7] That's why I don't want you thinking: "She's a post, so we only need to train post skills." Or: "She's a guard, so she doesn't need to work on anything inside." That's too limiting.
[¶8] DON'T TRAIN FOR THE PLAYER SHE IS TODAY. Train for the player she could become. If she's tall right now, don't assume she's done growing. If she's small right now, don't assume she will always be small. If she's playing center at 12 years old, that doesn't mean she'll play center in college. If she's the point guard today, that doesn't mean that's where a college coach will ultimately use her. The goal is to give her as many tools as possible before the game starts telling you where those tools fit best.
[¶9] And that's what we're going to dive into next. Because when we talk about college recruiting, we're not simply asking: "What position does she play?" We're asking: "What can she actually do?" And then: "How does that skill set fit into what a college program needs?" That's where position becomes much more interesting.
