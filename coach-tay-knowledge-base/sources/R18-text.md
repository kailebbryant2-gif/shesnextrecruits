[¶1] Let's clear something up: You do NOT have to play on an elite AAU team to get recruited. Players absolutely get recruited from their high school seasons. But there is a catch. It can be harder to get a college coach to your high school game. And understanding why will completely change the way you approach high school recruiting.
[¶2] WHY COLLEGE COACHES GO TO EXPOSURE EVENTS. Think about what an exposure tournament gives a college coach. They can walk into one gym and see: Multiple teams; Multiple players; Multiple positions; Multiple recruiting classes; Multiple players already on their recruiting board. It's basically a meeting place for recruiting. If a coach is traveling to an exposure event, they aren't going there to watch one player. They are going because there are a lot of potential players to evaluate in one place. That makes the trip worth their time.
[¶3] Now compare that to a random Tuesday night high school game. If you email a college coach who has never heard of you and say: "We're playing at 7:00 tonight. Come watch me." Why would that coach drive two hours to watch one player they've never seen? There isn't much reason for them to. That doesn't mean they don't want to see you. It means you haven't given them a good enough reason to make the trip.
[¶4] MAKE THE TRIP MAKE SENSE. This is one of the biggest things I want parents to understand about high school recruiting: You aren't just trying to get a coach interested in your daughter. You're trying to make it logical for the coach to come see her. That's especially important as you get outside of Division I. Division II, Division III, NAIA, and JUCO programs may have fewer recruiting resources, smaller staffs, smaller recruiting budgets, or less ability to travel to every game and event. They have to be strategic with where they spend their time.
[¶5] So think about it from the coach's perspective. If you're inviting a coach who lives four hours away to come watch one regular-season high school game... That's a difficult sell. But if you're inviting a coach who is 45 minutes away to a Christmas tournament where several teams are playing, and that coach already has two players on other teams they want to watch... Now the trip makes a lot more sense. And here's the kicker: They can watch those players AND watch your daughter while they're there. That's the opportunity you want to create.
[¶6] THE BEST HIGH SCHOOL OPPORTUNITY IS OFTEN A TOURNAMENT. High school tournaments can be extremely valuable for recruiting. Think about tournaments that happen: Early in the season; Around Thanksgiving; Around Christmas; During holiday breaks; At larger local or regional events. These events can give coaches a reason to be in the gym. And if you are trying to recruit a player through her high school season, that's exactly what you want. You want your game to be happening somewhere a coach already has a reason to go. This is the same concept we've talked about throughout this course: Make yourself easier to recruit. But now we're taking that one step further. Make the situation you're recruiting from make sense, too.
[¶7] LOCAL MATTERS. This is where your school list becomes really important. If you are primarily relying on your high school season for exposure, start by looking at schools within a realistic driving radius. Ask: Who could realistically come watch her play? A school 30–60 minutes away has a very different recruiting equation than a school four hours away. That doesn't mean a four-hour-away school can't recruit you. It means you shouldn't build your entire high school recruiting strategy around hoping a coach will make a long trip to watch one player they've never seen. Start where it makes sense. Local coaches have fewer barriers to seeing you. And if they already have another player they want to evaluate at the same tournament? Even better.
[¶8] BUT GETTING THEM THERE IS ONLY HALF THE JOB. This is where players sometimes make a mistake. They work really hard to get a coach into the gym... …and then forget that the reason the coach came was to evaluate them. When that coach gets there: You have to show them something. You need to demonstrate that you can actually do the things you've been telling them you can do. If you've told a coach you're a great shooter, they need to see you shoot. If you've told them you're a point guard, they need to see you handle pressure and make decisions. If you've told them you're a great defender, they need to see you defend. If you've told them you're versatile, they need to see you impact the game in multiple ways. The invitation gets them in the gym. Your performance gives them a reason to keep watching.
[¶9] ONE GOOD GAME IS NOT A RECRUITING PLAN. This is probably the most important part of this lesson. If high school is your primary exposure opportunity, consistency matters even more. Think about it from the coach's perspective. If they only have one realistic opportunity to see you because you're not playing in major AAU tournaments or showcase events... That game matters. But what if you have a great game? Then a bad game. Then another great game. Then disappear for three games. Then have 25 points against a weaker opponent. The coach still has questions. Who is the real player? You want your high school season to answer that question for them. They should be able to watch you over time and say: "This is who she is." Not: "I wonder if that was just a really good night."
[¶10] IDEALLY, YOUR HIGH SCHOOL AND AAU SEASONS WORK TOGETHER. The best scenario isn't necessarily: AAU OR high school. It's: AAU + high school. Your AAU season gives coaches additional opportunities to see you. Your high school season gives them additional opportunities to evaluate you. Your development continues through both. Your film continues to grow through both. Your relationships with coaches can continue through both. And ideally, each season reinforces what the other one showed. AAU gets them interested. High school confirms it. Or sometimes: High school gets them interested. AAU gives them another opportunity to evaluate you. Either way, you want the two seasons working together.
[¶11] IF HIGH SCHOOL IS YOUR MAIN EXPOSURE — YOU HAVE TO BE INTENTIONAL. Maybe AAU isn't financially realistic. Maybe you don't play for a major AAU program. Maybe your family has chosen to focus on local basketball. That's okay. But if high school and local events are going to be your primary recruiting exposure, you cannot treat them casually. You need to be intentional about: Which schools you contact; How far away those schools are; Which tournaments they are likely to attend; When you invite them; What information you send them; Your film; Your performance; Your consistency; Your communication; Your relationship with your high school coach.
[¶12] Don't just send: "Come watch me play." Give the coach a reason to come. "We're playing in a tournament this weekend. I know you have recruited several players from this event in the past, and I wanted to make sure you had our schedule. I'm wearing #12." That's a much more useful invitation.
[¶13] THE BIG IDEA. Getting recruited from high school is absolutely possible. But remember: College coaches have to decide where their time is worth spending. Your job is to make that decision easier. Get them to an event where they already have a reason to be. Make it easy for them to find you. Make sure they know your schedule. Then, when they get there... BE THE PLAYER THEY CAME TO SEE. And don't expect one great game to do all the work. If high school is your primary recruiting platform, consistency becomes your exposure. You want a coach to watch you once and think: "I like her." Watch you again: "She's consistent." Watch you again: "I know exactly what I'm getting." That's when high school basketball becomes a recruiting tool instead of just a basketball season.
